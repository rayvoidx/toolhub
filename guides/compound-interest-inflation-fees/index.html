Return, inflation and fees: what 30 years of compounding leaves

A compound interest calculator gives you one big number. Two quiet deductions decide how much of it is real: the fee taken every year, and the inflation that shrinks what each dollar buys. This guide applies both to one savings plan.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

First, check the method against a published example

The US Securities and Exchange Commission's investor bulletin How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio charts $100,000 growing at 4% a year for 20 years under ongoing fees of 0.25%, 0.50% and 1%. The bulletin gives the end values only as a chart, at roughly $208,000, $198,000 and $179,000. Taking the fee as a percentage of the balance after each year's growth, value = 100,000 × (1.04 × (1 − fee))20, reproduces them:

The SEC's point is in the last column: a 1% fee does not cost 1% of anything you would notice. It costs almost $40,000, because every dollar taken in year one would also have earned 19 more years of return. The fee compounds against you exactly as the return compounds for you.

The plan: $10,000 now, $500 a month, 30 years

Now a more typical saver. Start with $10,000, add $500 at the end of every month for 30 years, and assume a 7% annual return compounded monthly. The 7% is an assumption for illustration, not a forecast for any market. Over 30 years you put in $190,000. The compound interest calculator, which steps the balance month by month, ends at $691,150, so $501,150 of the total is growth.

Notice how late that growth arrives. After 10 years the balance is $106,639, after 20 years $300,851, and more than half of the final figure is added in the last decade. Anything that trims the rate, such as a fee, does its worst damage in exactly those late years, when the balance is largest.

Correction one: the fee

For monthly contributions it is simplest to subtract the fee from the return and run the calculator again with the net rate, for example 6% for a fund returning 7% with a 1% fee. Over a year this differs from the SEC's multiply-after-growth method by less than a tenth of a percentage point, which does not change any conclusion below.

A 1% fee removes $128,667, or 18.6% of what the plan would otherwise have reached, even though it is only one-seventh of the gross return. The loss grows with both the fee and the time each dollar stays invested, and the first $10,000 here stays for all 30 years. "Typical of a broad index fund" is a description of the fee level, not a recommendation; fund costs are disclosed in each fund's prospectus fee table, so read the actual expense ratio rather than relying on a category.

Correction two: inflation

$691,150 in 30 years will not buy what $691,150 buys today. To estimate how much less, look backwards. The US Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U, published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics) averaged 148.2 in 1994 and 313.7 in 2024. Prices rose by a factor of 2.117 in those 30 years, an average of 2.53% a year. The inflation calculator uses the same annual averages.

If the next 30 years look like the last 30, divide each ending balance by 2.117 to express it in today's dollars:

Inflation more than halves the headline. Fees and inflation then interact: with a 1% fee and 2.53% inflation, only about $266,000 of today's purchasing power is left from a number that started at $691,150. That is still well above the $190,000 contributed, but the gap between "what the calculator shows" and "what you can spend" is the size of a house.

Real returns are divided, not subtracted

The quick rule says real return = nominal return − inflation, so 7% − 2.53% = 4.47%. The exact relation, usually credited to Irving Fisher, divides: (1 + 0.07) ÷ (1 + 0.0253) − 1 = 4.36%. At low rates the two barely differ; at high inflation, the subtraction overstates real return badly.

The real rate also fixes a hidden flaw in the plan above. A flat $500 a month is worth less every year, so in today's money your contributions shrink. If instead you raise the deposit with inflation each year, which keeps its real value at $500, you can run the whole plan in today's dollars by entering the real rate: $10,000 plus $500 a month at 4.36% for 30 years gives $407,085 in today's money, against $326,517 for the flat plan. Indexing your contributions to prices adds more real wealth than cutting a 0.5% fee would.

Doubling and halving in your head

The rule of 72 estimates doubling time as 72 ÷ the annual rate. It works because ln 2 ≈ 0.693, and for small r, ln(1 + r) ≈ r; 72 is used instead of 69.3 because it divides evenly by many numbers and partly corrects the approximation at typical rates.

The rule is best near 8% and drifts at the extremes. It also runs in reverse for inflation: at 2.53% a year, the rule says prices double, and cash loses half its purchasing power, in about 28.4 years; the exact figure is 27.7 years. Cash under the mattress over a working life is not safe, just slow to lose.

Where the arithmetic misleads

  • Fixed rates are a modelling choice. Real returns arrive unevenly. A plan that averages 7% but loses heavily in the final years ends lower than the smooth calculation, because the losses hit the largest balance. Treat the output as a central case, not a promise.
  • Compounding frequency on a savings account is a minor detail. For a three-year plan of $500 a month deposited at the start of each month at 4%, the savings calculator shows $1,154 of interest with monthly compounding against $1,110 with simple interest, a difference of $44. Over three years the rate matters far more than how often it compounds.
  • Fees hide in more than one place. An advisory fee charged on top of a fund's expense ratio adds to it; 0.75% plus 0.25% is the 1% row above.
  • Taxes are not in any of these numbers. Whether growth is taxed yearly, on withdrawal or not at all depends on the account type and the country, and can move the result as much as the fee does.
  • Past CPI is not future CPI. 2.53% is a 30-year US average. Other countries, and other 30-year windows, differ a great deal.

What this guide does not cover

Investment selection, tax-advantaged accounts and their rules in any country, sequence-of-returns risk in retirement withdrawals, and currency effects for savings held abroad. The 7% return is an assumption, and the inflation figures are US data only.
