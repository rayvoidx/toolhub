Hourly pay, salary and overtime under US federal law

Turning $25 an hour into $52,000 a year is one multiplication. Getting overtime right is not: the federal rule is time and a half of a regular rate that can include bonuses, measured one workweek at a time. These worked examples use US federal law unless a state is named.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

Hourly to annual: where 2,080 comes from

A full-time US job is usually described as 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which is 2,080 paid hours a year. That figure is a convention, not a law, and it already assumes that every week, including holidays and vacation, is paid. The hourly to salary calculator multiplies hourly rate × hours per week × weeks per year, then divides by 12 for a monthly figure and by 24 for a semi-monthly one.

The gap between the first and last rows is $4,000 a year, purely from how many weeks you actually get paid. Contractors and hourly workers without paid leave should use their real number of paid weeks, not 52.

Going the other way, a $60,000 salary over 2,080 hours is $28.85 an hour. If the job really takes 45 hours a week, the same salary is $25.64 an hour. That second number is the one to compare against an hourly offer.

Monthly is not four weeks

A common shortcut is weekly pay × 4. At $25 an hour that gives $4,000, but a year has 52 weeks, not 48, so the average month is 52 ÷ 12 = 4.33 weeks and pays $4,333.33. If you are paid every two weeks you get 26 paychecks a year of $2,000.00 each; two months of the year contain three paydays. Budgeting from a two-paycheck month is safe; budgeting from biweekly pay × 2 understates your yearly income by one paycheck's worth twice a year.

The federal overtime rule in one sentence

Section 7(a)(1) of the Fair Labor Standards Act (29 U.S.C. 207) says a covered, non-exempt employee who works more than forty hours in a workweek must be paid for the extra hours “at a rate not less than one and one-half times the regular rate at which he is employed.” Three words in that sentence do most of the work: forty, workweek and regular rate.

Plain hourly worker. $20.00 an hour, 46 hours in one workweek.

40 × $20.00 + 6 × ($20.00 × 1.5) = $800.00 + $180.00 = $980.00

The overtime premium, the money above straight time, is 6 × $10.00 = $60.00. Spread across all 46 hours the week averages $21.30 an hour. This is what the overtime pay calculator returns with its default 40-hour threshold and 1.5 multiplier.

Workweek means one fixed week, never an average

The Department of Labor defines a workweek as “a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours — seven consecutive 24-hour periods,” and states that “averaging of hours over two or more weeks is not permitted.” The interpretive regulation, 29 CFR 778.104, uses the same example many payroll mistakes come from: 30 hours one week and 50 the next averages 40, but overtime is still owed for the second week.

The averaged paycheck is $100.00 short. Being paid biweekly or semi-monthly changes nothing: the regulation says the rule applies whether a worker is paid on a daily, weekly, biweekly or monthly basis. When you enter hours into a calculator, enter one workweek at a time and add the results. A timesheet calculator is useful here because it totals each week's clock-in and clock-out times before you decide which hours are overtime.

Regular rate: bonuses change the overtime rate

The “regular rate” is not automatically the hourly rate on your offer letter. The Labor Department's overtime fact sheet says the regular rate “includes all remuneration for employment except certain payments excluded by the Act itself.” Section 7(e) lists the exclusions: gifts, pay for time not worked such as vacation or holidays, expense reimbursements, and discretionary bonuses, meaning bonuses where both the decision to pay and the amount are left to the employer until near the end of the period and were not promised in advance. A production bonus, attendance bonus or shift incentive you were told about in advance is not discretionary, so it goes into the regular rate.

The method in 29 CFR 778.110(b) is: add all straight-time earnings for the week, including the bonus, divide by all hours worked, then pay an extra half of that rate for each overtime hour (the straight-time part of those hours is already in the total). The regulation's own example is $12 an hour plus a $46 bonus over 46 hours, which gives a regular rate of $13.00 and total pay of $637.00.

Same worker, now with a $100 weekly attendance bonus. $20.00 an hour, 46 hours.

Straight time: 46 × $20.00 + $100.00 = $1,020.00
Regular rate: $1,020.00 ÷ 46 = $22.17
Overtime premium: 6 × ½ × $22.17 = $66.52
Total: $1,086.52

Paying the bonus on top of the $980.00 calculated earlier gives $1,080.00, which is $6.52 short for the week. Over 50 such weeks that is roughly $326.

A general-purpose overtime calculator asks for one hourly rate, so it cannot see the bonus. Work out the regular rate first, then the premium, as above.

Two different hourly rates in one week

Someone who works two jobs for the same employer at different rates has a weighted-average regular rate. California's Labor Commissioner illustrates it with 32 hours at $11.00 and 10 hours at $9.00 in one workweek: $442.00 over 42 hours is a regular rate of $10.52. Under the federal 40-hour rule the two overtime hours would then earn an extra half of that rate, $10.52 in total, for a week's pay of $452.52.

A fixed salary for a non-exempt employee

Salaried does not mean exempt. The fact sheet's example is a worker hired for a 45-hour week at $405: the regular rate is $405 ÷ 45 = $9.00. Because the salary already covers straight time for all 45 hours, the five overtime hours add only the half-time premium, 5 × $4.50 = $22.50, for $427.50 that week. If the salary was meant to cover only 40 hours, the regular rate and the result are different, which is why the agreement on what the salary covers matters.

What federal law does not require

The fact sheet is explicit that the FLSA “does not require overtime pay for work on Saturdays, Sundays, holidays, or regular days of rest, as such.” Weekend and holiday premiums are a matter of contract or company policy. Federal law also has no daily overtime: a worker can do four 10-hour days and receive no federal overtime, because the week is exactly 40 hours.

State law can be stricter: California as an example

States may set higher standards than the FLSA, and when both apply the worker gets whichever is more generous. California is the best-known case. According to the state's Division of Labor Standards Enforcement, non-exempt employees there earn time and a half for hours over 8 in a workday and over 40 in a workweek, and for the first 8 hours on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek, and double time for hours over 12 in a workday and over 8 on that seventh day. Exceptions exist for some schedules and industries, so check the state's own guidance before relying on this.

Checks before you trust a number

  • Is the job exempt? Overtime applies only to non-exempt employees. Exemption depends on duties and salary tests that this guide does not try to apply.
  • Is it one workweek? Split two-week totals into the employer's fixed workweeks before calculating.
  • Were any bonuses promised? If yes, rebuild the regular rate. Holiday gifts and pay for vacation hours stay out of it.
  • Which state? Daily overtime, seventh-day rules and higher minimum wages are set by states and sometimes cities.
  • Gross or take-home? Everything above is gross pay. A paycheck calculator estimates federal income tax and FICA withholding from it; state income tax varies by state.

What this guide does not cover
