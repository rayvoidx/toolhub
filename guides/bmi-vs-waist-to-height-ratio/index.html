BMI vs waist-to-height ratio: what each number misses

BMI is a weight-for-height number. Waist-to-height ratio is a fat-location number. They answer different questions, and the interesting cases are the ones where they disagree.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

Two formulas, two questions

Body mass index divides weight in kilograms by height in metres squared. Nothing else goes in: not age, sex, muscle, bone, or where fat sits on the body. A 175 cm person weighing 82 kg has a BMI of 82 ÷ 1.75² = 26.78 whether that weight is in the shoulders and thighs or around the abdomen.

Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) divides waist circumference by height, both in the same unit. It ignores total weight entirely. What it captures is central (abdominal) fat, which is the fat most closely tied to type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease. That is why the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) now asks clinicians to record both: in its guideline NG246 it says to use BMI as a practical measure of overweight and obesity but to "interpret it with caution because it is not a direct measure of central adiposity", and to add waist-to-height ratio for adults with a BMI below 35.

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

WHtR = waist ÷ height (same unit)

NICE's own worked example: a 96.5 cm waist and 170 cm height give 96.5 ÷ 170 = 0.5676, which rounds to 0.57. In inches, 38 ÷ 67 = 0.5672, also 0.57. The unit cancels, so either works — as long as both numbers use the same one.

The second and third rows look like they should match, and they do not. The Western Pacific report, The Asia-Pacific perspective (2000), proposed 23 for overweight and 25 for obesity as provisional values and said they would need revising as more studies came in. In 2004 a WHO expert consultation published in The Lancet found that the BMI at which risk rises varies from 22 to 25 across Asian populations, kept the standard WHO cut-offs as the international classification, and added "public health action points" at 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5. NICE's 2022 recommendation follows the 23 / 27.5 pattern and applies it to a wider group than East Asia. The 2000 report also notes that its lower cut-offs do not apply to Pacific Islanders, for whom it suggests higher ones.

So "BMI 26" can be honestly reported as overweight (WHO, and NICE for the groups in 1.9.11) or obese class I (WHO Western Pacific 2000). Neither label is wrong; check which rule book an app used before comparing its words with another.

Waist-to-height ratio has one set of bands in NICE NG246 (recommendation 1.9.14), and NICE says it applies to both sexes, all ethnicities, and adults with high muscle mass, as long as BMI is under 35:

The practical rule NICE gives for talking to patients is short: keep your waist to less than half your height. For a 175 cm adult that is a waist under 87.5 cm; at 160 cm it is under 80 cm.

A: BMI says overweight, the waist says otherwise

Person A's BMI of 26.78 is overweight under WHO rules and obese class I under the Western Pacific bands. His waist of 81 cm is well under half his 175 cm height, giving 0.463. A Navy-method estimate with a 40 cm neck puts him at about 12.0% body fat, which the body fat tool files under "athletes" (6–14% for men). This is the case NICE has in mind in recommendation 1.9.12: interpret BMI with caution in adults with high muscle mass. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat.

B: the threshold you pick decides the verdict

Person B sits at 23.51. Under WHO cut-offs that is comfortably normal. Under the Western Pacific bands it is "at risk", and under NICE 1.9.11 — if he has, say, a South Asian or Chinese family background — it is overweight. His waist tells a consistent story with the lower thresholds: 88 ÷ 165 = 0.533, in NICE's increased band, and his waist-to-hip ratio of 0.917 (hips 96 cm) is at or above the 0.90 line for men in the table of cut-offs commonly attributed to WHO (more on that table below). This is what the lower thresholds were written for.

C: normal BMI, central fat

Person C's BMI of 22.66 is normal on every chart in this guide. Her waist is 84 cm, which is more than half her height (0.525) and above the 80 cm waist line for women in the same commonly cited table. Her waist-to-hip ratio is 84 ÷ 98 = 0.857, which rounds to 0.86 and crosses the 0.85 "substantially increased" line for women. NICE flags this exact situation in 1.9.10: a person in the healthy-weight BMI category "may nevertheless have central adiposity". If you only looked at BMI you would never know.

D: both numbers agree

Person D is overweight by BMI (27.17) and in the increased band by waist (0.571), with a waist-to-hip ratio of exactly 1.00. When both point the same way, the second adds confidence more than information, which is also why NICE does not ask for WHtR above a BMI of 35: its committee noted that people in that range are almost always going to have a high ratio anyway.

Why waist-to-height and not waist alone?

A fixed waist cut-off treats a 155 cm person and a 190 cm person the same. The waist thresholds most calculators use — 94 and 102 cm for men, 80 and 88 cm for women, plus waist-to-hip ratios of 0.90 and 0.85 — are usually described as WHO recommendations. The WHO's own 2008 expert consultation report is more careful: it explains that the waist figures came from a Dutch sample of 2,183 men and 2,698 women aged 20–59, were printed in a 2000 WHO report "as an example only and not WHO recommendations", and that other bodies use different lines (the International Diabetes Federation, for instance, uses above 90 cm for South Asian, Chinese and Japanese men). Our waist-to-hip tool uses this common table, so treat its bands as a convention rather than a WHO ruling. Dividing by height removes most of that bookkeeping. A 2012 meta-analysis by Ashwell, Gunn and Gibson in Obesity Reviews, pooling 31 papers covering more than 300,000 adults, concluded that WHtR was better than waist circumference and BMI at detecting cardiometabolic risk factors in both sexes and should be considered as a screening tool.

What "ideal weight" formulas add (and do not)

Ideal-weight formulas are height-only, like BMI, but they disagree with each other more than people expect. For a 175 cm man with a medium frame, our ideal weight calculator returns 70.5 kg (Devine), 68.9 kg (Robinson), 68.7 kg (Miller) and 72.0 kg (Hamwi): a 3.3 kg spread from the same input. The BMI 18.5–24.9 range for the same height is 56.7 to 76.3 kg, almost 20 kg wide. For a 160 cm woman the formulas give 52.4, 54.1, 57.2 and 52.1 kg, against a BMI range of 47.4 to 63.7 kg. None of them can tell Person A from Person D.

Measurement mistakes the calculators cannot catch

  • Mixed units. A 34-inch waist divided by a 170 cm height gives 0.2, which looks like an unusually lean result rather than an error. Convert first; WHtR only cancels units when both numbers share one.
  • Wrong spot on the waist. NICE's method is midway between the bottom of the ribs and the top of the hip bones, just above the belly button, after breathing out normally. Measuring at the narrowest point or over clothing makes the ratio look better than it is. The Navy body-fat method measures men at the navel, so the two tools can legitimately get different waist numbers from the same body.
  • Swapped waist and hip. The waist-to-hip tool warns when the waist is more than 1.5 times the hip, but a smaller swap — 98 for the waist and 84 for the hip — just produces a wrong ratio without any warning.
  • Height from memory. Self-reported height tends to be the number from years ago. A 2 cm overstatement at 165 cm lowers BMI by about 2.4% and WHtR by about 1.2%; that is enough to move someone across a band edge such as 23.
  • Rounding at the edge. The waist-to-hip tool rounds the ratio to two decimals before choosing a band, so 0.8469 shows as 0.85 and is labelled high. A value displayed on a boundary is a coin flip, not a verdict.

Using both numbers together

Check which BMI thresholds apply to you — the global WHO set, or a lower set if a population named by WPRO or NICE describes your background — then check whether your waist is under half your height. If both are in range, a tape and a scale have little more to say. If they disagree, the disagreement itself is the useful finding to take to a clinician, who can measure what a tape cannot: blood pressure, glucose and lipids.

What this guide does not cover

Children and teenagers (BMI is read against age- and sex-specific growth charts, not these adult bands), pregnancy, adults over 65 (NICE 1.9.13 asks for extra caution), and anyone with a BMI of 35 or more, for whom NICE does not use waist-to-height ratio. It is not a diagnosis: these thresholds are screening lines published by WHO and NICE, and only a clinician can say what your numbers mean for you.
