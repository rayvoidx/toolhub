APR vs APY, and what a credit card balance really costs

Banks quote the number that flatters the product: APY when they pay you, APR when you pay them. Converting between the two, and then following a real card balance month by month, shows how far apart the headline and the cost can be.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

Two names for one rate, measured differently

An annual percentage rate (APR) is a nominal rate: the rate per compounding period multiplied by the number of periods in a year. An annual percentage yield (APY) is what that rate actually produces over a year once interest earns interest. With n compounding periods a year,

APY = (1 + APR ÷ n)n − 1   and back again   APR = n × ((1 + APY)1/n − 1)

With continuous compounding the limit is APY = eAPR − 1. These are the formulas in the APY calculator and the APR to APY calculator.

In the US, the two terms are fixed by different regulations. For deposit accounts, the Truth in Savings Act's Regulation DD (12 CFR part 1030, Appendix A) defines APY from the interest actually paid: APY = 100 × ((1 + Interest ÷ Principal)365 ÷ days in term − 1), which for a one-year account reduces to interest ÷ principal. For credit, the Truth in Lending Act's Regulation Z requires the APR, and on a credit card that is simply the periodic rate scaled to a year. A card is never advertised with its compounded equivalent.

At a savings rate, compounding frequency is worth about a tenth of a percentage point, and going from daily to continuous adds almost nothing. At a card rate the effect is more than twenty times larger: 22.36% compounded daily costs like 25.05% simple. The 22.36% is the Federal Reserve's average rate on US credit card accounts assessed interest for the third quarter of 2026 (G.19 release, 6 November 2026), so this is an ordinary card, not an extreme one.

The reverse direction catches people comparing savings offers. An account advertising 4.60% APY with daily compounding has an underlying rate of 4.498%; with monthly compounding, 4.506%. If one bank quotes APR and another APY, convert one of them before deciding which pays more.

What one month of card interest costs

US card issuers usually work with a daily periodic rate, the APR divided by 365, applied to each day's balance. On a steady $3,000 balance at 22.36%:

Daily rate = 22.36% ÷ 365 = 0.06126% · Interest for a 30-day cycle = $3,000 × 0.0006126 × 30 = $55.13

A 31-day cycle costs $56.97. The credit card interest calculator does this for any balance and cycle length.

Two details the calculator cannot see. First, the grace period: on most cards, if you paid last month's statement balance in full, new purchases accrue no interest at all. Carry any balance and you usually lose the grace period, so new purchases start accruing from the day they post. Second, the real balance moves during the month. Issuers average the daily balances, so a purchase on day 2 costs almost a full cycle of interest and a payment on day 2 saves almost a full cycle. Paying early in the cycle, not just on the due date, lowers the cost.

The minimum-payment trap, in numbers

Since the US Credit CARD Act of 2009, 15 U.S.C. §1637(b)(11) requires every statement to carry a "Minimum Payment Warning" and show how many months paying only the minimum would take, the total cost, and the monthly payment that would clear the balance in 36 months. The CFPB points out that these figures assume no new purchases. Here is why the warning exists, for a $5,000 balance at 22.36% with no new spending. The calculator's month-by-month simulation applies one-twelfth of the APR each month.

The first row is the trap in its purest form. A month's interest is 22.36% ÷ 12 = 1.863% of the balance, so a 2% minimum repays only about 0.137% of the balance each month. After 600 payments totalling $40,962, $2,200.92 is still owed. Many issuers instead set the minimum as 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, as in the second row, so that every payment reduces the principal. Even so, that takes more than 19 years.

The fourth row is the cheapest way out of this table that does not require more money in the first month. $150 a month as a fixed amount clears the debt in 53 months; 3% of the balance, which also starts at $150, takes 210 months. The difference is that the percentage payment shrinks as the balance shrinks, so you stop paying down principal at the rate you started. Deciding on a fixed dollar amount and keeping it there, even as the statement's minimum falls, is worth $4,426 here.

Several debts: avalanche or snowball?

With more than one balance, you pay every minimum and put whatever is left on one target. The avalanche method targets the highest APR first; the snowball method targets the smallest balance first. The debt payoff calculator runs both on the same budget.

Three debts, minimums of $450 plus $300 extra = $750 a month

  • Avalanche (Visa, then store card, then car loan): debt-free in 25 months, $2,424.16 total interest.
  • Snowball (store card, then Visa, then car loan): debt-free in 25 months, $2,518.46 total interest.
  • Minimums only, with no extra $300: 52 months, $7,582 interest.

Avalanche is mathematically optimal and saves $94.30 here; snowball pays off a whole account first, which some people need in order to keep going. When the smallest balance also carries the highest rate, the two methods are the same plan. Either way the larger effect is the $300 extra itself: it cuts the time from 52 to 25 months and saves more than $5,000.

Checks before you trust any of these numbers

  • Several APRs on one card. Purchases, cash advances and balance transfers often carry different rates, and a promotional 0% transfer rate ends on a fixed date. Model each balance separately.
  • Penalty APR. A late payment can trigger a much higher rate on future transactions. Check your cardholder agreement for the rate and the conditions.
  • Fees are not in the APR on a card statement. Annual fees, late fees and transfer fees add to the cost but not to the purchase APR.
  • Rates move. Most US card APRs are variable and follow the prime rate, so a 36-month plan made today can cost more if rates rise.

What this guide does not cover
