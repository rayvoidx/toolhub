15, 20 or 30 years: what a fixed-rate loan really costs

The monthly payment is the number lenders lead with, but the term and what you do after signing decide the total. Here is one $300,000 loan followed through every common choice, using the same formulas our calculators run.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

The rates used here

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey for the week of 19 November 2026 put the average US 30-year fixed rate at 7.28% and the 15-year at 6.66%. Those two numbers are used below. Freddie Mac does not publish a 20-year average, so the 20-year row uses an illustrative 7.00%, between the two. Your own quote will differ with credit score, down payment, points and the date you lock; the comparisons hold anyway, because they depend on the gap between terms, not on the level.

Three terms, one loan

Every fixed-rate loan on this site is priced with the standard annuity formula, payment = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1), where P is the amount borrowed, r is the annual rate divided by 12, and n is the number of monthly payments. The formula simply finds the level payment whose present value, discounted at the loan rate, equals the cash you received.

The 15-year payment is $587.14 a month higher than the 30-year one, about 29% more, yet the total interest is $263,789 lower, a cut of 60%. Two forces stack: the shorter loan carries a lower rate, and the balance falls far faster, so there is less principal outstanding for interest to accrue on.

The second force is easy to underestimate. On the 30-year loan, the first twelve payments total $24,632, of which only $2,887 reduces the balance; the other $21,745 is interest. After three full years you still owe $290,672, and after five years $283,224. Early in a long loan, almost the whole payment is rent on the money.

Paying extra on a 30-year loan

An extra principal payment skips the rest of the schedule: every dollar you prepay stops accruing 7.28% for all the remaining months. The mortgage calculator simulates this month by month, and these are its results for the same 30-year loan.

The first $100 does the most work per dollar. Going from $100 to $500 a month quintuples the extra outlay but less than triples the saving, because the faster you pay, the fewer remaining months there are for each prepaid dollar to escape.

Should you just take the 15-year loan?

Try paying the 15-year payment on the 30-year loan, which means $587.14 extra each month. The loan is gone after 194 months, about 16 years and 2 months, with $210,389 of interest. The genuine 15-year loan finishes a year and two months sooner with $175,161 of interest, so the lower rate is worth $35,229.

What the extra $35,229 buys is flexibility. With the 30-year loan, the required payment stays at $2,052.64; in a bad year you can stop the extra and owe nothing more. With the 15-year loan, $2,639.78 is a contractual obligation. If your income is steady and you have an emergency fund, the shorter term is cheaper. If not, the option can be worth paying for. Neither the calculator nor this guide can price that risk for you.

Biweekly payments: one extra payment a year

Paying half the monthly amount every two weeks gives 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 monthly payments instead of 12. The biweekly mortgage calculator models the thirteenth payment as one-twelfth extra each month, a close and slightly conservative stand-in for the real schedule.

Half-payment: $2,052.64 ÷ 2 = $1,026.32 every two weeks

Paid off after 283 months (23 years 7 months) with $327,318 of interest, which saves $111,631. The same result comes from adding $171.05 a month (one-twelfth of the payment) to a normal monthly schedule.

That equivalence matters, because some companies sell biweekly "programs" for a fee. In 2015 the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sued one such company, Nationwide Biweekly Administration, alleging it charged setup fees of up to $995 plus $84 to $101 a year, and that the first extra payments went to the setup fee rather than the loan. If your servicer accepts extra principal for free, a $171.05 monthly prepayment does the same job with no middleman. Check also that your servicer applies a half-payment when it arrives; some hold partial payments until a full one has accumulated, which removes most of the benefit.

When refinancing pays back

Suppose that three years in, with $290,672 left and 27 years to go, rates drop and you are offered 6.28%, one point lower. Assume $6,000 of closing costs; that figure is illustrative, so use the total from your own Loan Estimate. The refinance calculator divides costs by the monthly saving to get a break-even month, and separately compares lifetime interest including the costs.

The new 30-year loan looks best on the monthly-saving and break-even columns and worst on the lifetime column. It restarts the clock: you would be making payments for 33 years in total instead of 30, and those extra three years eat most of the rate cut. Matching the remaining term is the honest comparison. The 15-year option raises the payment, so there is nothing to "break even" on, but it removes almost $200,000 of future interest.

A simple break-even month also ignores two things. First, if you sell or refinance again before it, you lose money. Second, the costs are often rolled into the new balance, in which case you pay interest on them too. If the lender offers a "no-cost" refinance, the cost is usually in a higher rate; compare the rate you would get with and without the credit.

Mistakes the calculators cannot catch

  • Comparing payments that include different things. All figures here are principal and interest. A lender's or servicer's quote often adds property tax, homeowners insurance and mortgage insurance (escrow). Strip those out before comparing terms, because they do not change with the term.
  • Rate versus APR. The calculators take the note rate. The APR on a US Loan Estimate also spreads points and some fees over the loan, so two offers with the same rate can have different APRs. Use the rate for the payment, and the APR or the itemised costs for comparing offers.
  • Prepayment penalties. The CFPB notes that a penalty typically applies when you pay off the whole balance (by selling or refinancing) within a set period, usually three or five years, and normally not to small extra principal payments. Read your loan documents before planning a refinance in the early years.
  • Extra money that is not applied to principal. Label prepayments as principal-only and check the next statement. Money credited to a future payment instead does not shorten the loan.
  • Prepaying instead of keeping cash. Home equity is hard to get back out in an emergency. Build reserves first; the guide to compound interest, inflation and fees shows what that cash might earn instead.

What this guide does not cover

Adjustable-rate and interest-only mortgages, mortgage insurance, US tax treatment of mortgage interest, and loans outside the US, where terms, rate conventions (for example, semi-annual compounding on Canadian fixed-rate mortgages) and early-repayment rules differ. It also does not judge whether you can afford a given payment.
