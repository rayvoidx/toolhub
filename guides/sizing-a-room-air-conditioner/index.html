Sizing a room air conditioner: BTU, watts and running cost

Room air conditioners are sold by cooling capacity in BTU per hour, rated for efficiency by CEER, and billed by the kilowatt-hour. This guide connects the three with worked rooms, using US federal and ENERGY STAR figures.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

Three units for one machine

A label that says “12,000 BTU” means the unit can remove 12,000 British thermal units of heat from the room every hour. It is a rate, so it is properly written BTU/h. Two other units describe the same rate:

One ton is 12,000 BTU/h, a figure that goes back to the heat absorbed by melting a ton of ice in a day. Outside the US, capacity is usually printed in kilowatts, so a 3.5 kW split unit is roughly a 12,000 BTU/h one.

The trap is that cooling watts are not electrical watts. A 12,000 BTU/h unit moves about 3,517 W of heat but draws far less from the socket, because it pumps heat rather than making cold. How much less is what the efficiency rating tells you, covered below. If you put 3,517 W into an electricity cost calculator you will overstate the bill about four times.

The ENERGY STAR chart, and how it differs from 20 BTU per square foot

ENERGY STAR publishes a step chart that maps floor area to capacity, calculated for an 8-foot ceiling, with four adjustments: reduce capacity by 10% for a heavily shaded room, increase it by 10% for a very sunny one, add 600 BTU/h for each regular occupant beyond two, and add 4,000 BTU/h if the unit is in a kitchen.

Many online calculators, including the BTU calculator here, use a smooth rule instead: 20 BTU/h per square foot, scaled by ceiling height relative to 8 feet, then the same sun, people and kitchen adjustments, rounded to the nearest 500. Dividing each chart step by the middle of its area range shows where the two agree:

The flat 20 BTU/h rule lands close to the chart between roughly 500 and 1,200 square feet, but it falls well below the chart for small rooms. That is partly physics (a small room has more wall and window per square foot of floor) and partly the market: the chart starts at 5,000 BTU/h, which is about the smallest window unit sold. For a bedroom, treat the 20 BTU/h result as a floor and read the chart.

Notes on the arithmetic:

  • Room B, chart: 6,000 × 1.1 = 6,600, plus 2 × 600 for the third and fourth person = 7,800, so you would shop for an 8,000 BTU/h unit. The rule gives 224 × 20 × 1.1 + 1,200 = 6,128, which rounds to 6,000.
  • Room C shows why the kitchen addition dominates small spaces: 4,000 BTU/h is a third of the 12,000 base.
  • Room D: the chart has no ceiling-height rule; ENERGY STAR only says its figures assume 8 feet. The calculator scales by 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25, giving 180 × 20 × 1.25 × 0.9 = 4,050, rounded to 4,000. If you have a tall ceiling, the chart figure is the one more likely to be short.
  • Room E is the metric case: 20 m² is 215.3 sq ft. In kilowatts the chart's 6,000 BTU/h is 1.76 kW.
  • Boundaries: rows read “450 up to 550” and “550 up to 700”. A room of exactly 550 sq ft sits on the line between 12,000 and 14,000; if it is sunny or busy, take the higher one.

The calculator also offers a hot-climate (+15%) and very-hot-or-humid (+25%) multiplier. That is the calculator's own allowance, not part of the ENERGY STAR chart, so do not apply it on top of a chart figure you have already adjusted for sun.

Why bigger is not safer

The instinct is to round up generously. ENERGY STAR warns that an oversized unit “costs more to purchase, wastes energy and does not provide better cooling.” The Department of Energy's purchasing guidance for federal buyers adds the mechanism: oversizing leads to higher energy use and poor humidity removal because of excessive on-off cycling. An air conditioner dries the air only while its coil runs cold for a while. A unit that hits the thermostat setting in a few minutes and switches off leaves the room cool and clammy.

So round up to the next size sold, not two sizes. For room B, an 8,000 BTU/h unit fits; a 12,000 one is the mistake the warnings describe.

From CEER to a yearly bill

US room air conditioners are rated by CEER, expressed in BTU of cooling per watt-hour of electricity. That makes the energy arithmetic short:

annual kWh = capacity (BTU/h) ÷ CEER × hours of use ÷ 1,000

The Federal Energy Management Program's purchasing table for a 12,000 BTU/h unit assumes 750 hours a year and lists three efficiency levels. The formula reproduces its energy column exactly; the cost column below uses the US average residential electricity price for August 2026, 18.16 cents per kWh, from the EIA's Electric Power Monthly (preliminary).

Using an appliance cost calculator correctly

The electricity cost calculator multiplies watts × hours × days by your rate. A 12,000 BTU/h unit at CEER 14.7 draws about 816 W while the compressor runs. Entered as 816 W for 8 hours a day, every day, at $0.1816, it returns about 199 kWh and $36.09 a month.

That is an upper bound, because the calculator assumes full power for every hour the unit is switched on. A correctly sized unit cycles its compressor, and the 750-hour FEMP figure counts compressor running hours across a season. If you have a plug-in power meter, measure a typical day's kWh and use that instead of nameplate watts.

Converting between energy units also helps when a utility bills in something other than kWh or a spec sheet lists heat in BTU: one kilowatt-hour is 3,412 BTU, so the 612 kWh of electricity above is about 2.1 million BTU, while the unit removed 12,000 × 750 = 9 million BTU of heat from the room. The energy converter and power converter do these conversions.

Things no calculator can see

  • Air leaks around the unit. Gaps around a window unit or a portable unit's hose bring hot air straight back in.
  • Portable units. The FEMP purchasing requirements explicitly exclude portable air conditioners, which are rated differently; compare them with each other, not with window units of the same BTU figure.
  • Heat sources. A gaming PC, a home office full of screens or a south-facing glass wall can add more heat than an extra person.
  • Whole homes. A step chart is built for single rooms. Central systems are sized with a room-by-room load calculation that accounts for insulation, windows and duct losses.

What this guide does not cover

Central air and heat pump sizing (which needs a proper load calculation), heating capacity of reverse-cycle units, efficiency ratings used outside the US (such as SEER in Europe or APF in Japan), dehumidifier sizing, and electrical circuit requirements for larger units. Prices are a US national average for one month; your utility's rate and time-of-use pricing will differ.
