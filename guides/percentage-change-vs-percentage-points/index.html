Percentage change vs percentage points, loss recovery and margin math

Three everyday errors share the same root cause: dividing by the wrong base. Moving from 4% to 6% is either 2 percentage points or 50%, a 20% gain followed by a 20% drop loses 4%, and a 25% markup is only a 20% gross margin.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 28 November 2026 · Updated 28 November 2026

Why +20% followed by −20% is −4%, not break-even

Every percentage step multiplies the value immediately before it. Start with $100.00 in our percentage calculator: a 20% increase adds $20.00 to reach $120.00. A 20% decrease next takes one-fifth of $120.00 — $24.00, not $20.00 — leaving $96.00, a net −4.00% loss. Reversing the sequence gives the identical answer: −20% takes $100.00 down to $80.00, and +20% of $80.00 adds $16.00 to finish at $96.00.

(1 + r) × (1 − r) = 1 − r²

Equal percentage gains and losses always leave a net loss of r². At r = 20% (0.20), r² = 0.04, so 4.00% disappears; at r = 50% (0.50), r² = 0.25, leaving $75.00 (−25.00%).

To get back to the original starting value after a drop of d, the recovery gain has to act on the smaller base 1 − d, so the required gain is d ÷ (1 − d):

Percentage change vs percentage points (and basis points)

When the number moving is already a percentage — an interest rate, a tax rate, an unemployment rate, or a website conversion rate — subtracting the two figures gives percentage points, whereas dividing the change by the starting rate gives a relative percentage change. Both the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and the UK Office for National Statistics specify writing "percentage points" in full whenever you subtract two rates, because writing "rose by 2%" is ambiguous.

In bond and mortgage markets, 1 percentage point is split into 100 basis points (bps) so traders never confuse an additive move with a relative one. Moving a mortgage rate from 4.0% to 6.0% is +2.0 percentage points (+200 bps), which increases the interest rate itself by +50.0% relative to 4.0%:

Markup vs gross margin: same dollar profit, two denominators

Retailers and freelancers run into the exact same base shift when pricing goods. Markup divides gross profit by unit cost; gross margin divides the same dollar profit by selling price. Because selling price is larger than cost whenever you make a profit, gross margin is always a smaller percentage than markup.

Take a product that costs $80.00 to make or buy, and run five pricing targets through our margin and markup calculator:

Confusing the two is expensive when you run a sale. If you mark an $80.00 item up by 25.0% to $100.00 believing you have a "25% cushion", and then run a 20% storewide promotion in our discount calculator, 20% off $100.00 cuts $20.00 from the ticket — dropping revenue right back to $80.00 and wiping out 100% of your gross profit ($0.00 left). Discounts come off selling price, so your maximum break-even discount equals your gross margin (20.0%), never your markup (25.0%). If you need a true 25.0% gross margin on $80.00 cost, divide cost by (1 − 0.25) = $106.67, which is a 33.3% markup.

Quick sanity rules before quoting a percentage

  • Name the denominator whenever a rate changes. Write "up 2.0 percentage points (from 4.0% to 6.0%, a 50.0% relative jump)" so neither reading can mislead.
  • Never average multi-year percentage returns with a simple mean. A +50% year followed by a −50% year has an arithmetic average of 0%, yet $100.00 becomes $75.00 (−25.00% cumulative).
  • Compare discount rates against gross margin, not markup. Any promotional % off that exceeds gross margin sells every unit below cost.
