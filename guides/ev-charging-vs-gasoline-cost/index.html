EV charging vs gasoline: cost per 100 miles, worked out

Whether an electric car is cheaper to fuel comes down to two numbers per 100 miles and one price each. The answer flips depending on where you charge. Prices here are US averages from the EIA where stated, and labelled as illustrative where no national average exists.

By Jaehyun Park (RAYV STUDIO), who builds and maintains Toolhub · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

One line for each car

Put both cars on the same basis, cost per 100 miles:

Gasoline: 100 ÷ mpg × price per gallon
Electric: kWh per 100 miles × price per kWh

Everything else in this guide is about getting the inputs right. For prices, the US Energy Information Administration reported an average retail price for regular gasoline of $3.805 per gallon for the week of 16 November 2026, and an average residential electricity price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026 (preliminary). Both vary widely by state and month; substitute your own.

The break-even electricity price

Divide the gasoline car's cost per 100 miles by the EV's kWh per 100 miles. The result is the electricity price at which the two cost the same to drive.

Where the kWh figure comes from, and charging losses

The EPA window sticker lists electricity consumption in kWh per 100 miles, described as how many kilowatt-hours the vehicle would use to travel 100 miles. Your car's trip computer reports a figure too, usually in miles per kWh or kWh per 100 miles, but it measures energy leaving the battery. The meter on your house measures energy going into the charger, and some of that is lost as heat converting AC from the grid to DC for the battery.

How much? The Department of Energy's fueleconomy.gov puts charging losses at 10% of energy in combined driving in its energy-flow breakdown (16% in its city example), and says battery charging efficiency is often 84% to 93% in published studies. So convert a dashboard figure before you price it:

The trip computer shows 27 kWh per 100 miles. At 90% charging efficiency:

27 ÷ 0.90 = 30.0 kWh per 100 miles from the wall
30.0 × $0.1816 = $5.45 per 100 miles

Pricing the dashboard figure directly gives $4.90, about 10% too low.

The EV charging calculator keeps these two quantities apart. It computes energy into the battery from capacity and state of charge, energy from the wall by dividing by charging efficiency (90% unless you change it), cost from the wall energy and added range from the battery energy. So the miles-per-kWh you give it should be the battery-side figure from your dashboard.

One charging session. 75 kWh battery, 20% to 80%, 7.2 kW home charger, 90% efficiency, 3.5 mi/kWh, 18.16¢/kWh.

Into the battery: 75 × 60% = 45 kWh
From the wall: 45 ÷ 0.90 = 50.0 kWh
Time: 50.0 ÷ 7.2 kW = 6 h 57 min
Added range: 45 × 3.5 = 158 mi
Cost: 50.0 × $0.1816 = $9.08

At a DC fast charger the time arithmetic breaks down above about 80%: the calculator warns that it assumes constant power, while real fast charging slows sharply as the battery fills.

What MPGe does and does not tell you

MPGe, miles per gallon equivalent, converts electricity into gasoline gallons by energy content. The DOE uses the EPA window-sticker conversion of 33.7 kWh to 1 gallon of gasoline. An EV that uses 30 kWh per 100 miles is rated about 112.3 MPGe.

MPGe is useful for comparing how efficiently two cars use energy. It is not a cost figure, because a kWh and a thirty-third of a gallon do not cost the same. To get money back out of MPGe:

cost per 100 mi = 100 × 33.7 ÷ MPGe × price per kWh

For 112.3 MPGe at 18.16¢ that is $5.45, the same as above. Comparing 112.3 MPGe with 30 mpg and concluding the EV is 3.7 times cheaper to run is the most common misreading; at home prices it is about 2.3 times cheaper, and at the illustrative fast-charging price it is not cheaper at all.

Measuring your own numbers

Label figures are a starting point. For the gasoline car, fill the tank, reset the trip meter, and at the next fill divide miles by gallons; the MPG calculator does this and converts the result. For example, 342 miles on 11.4 gallons is 30.0 mpg, which is 7.84 L/100 km or 36.0 mpg in imperial gallons. If your figures arrive in other units, the fuel economy converter handles L/100 km, km/L and both kinds of mpg. European EV specifications often give kWh per 100 km: 18 kWh/100 km is 28.97 kWh per 100 miles.

For the EV, use the kWh your home charger or utility app reports over a month, divided by the miles driven. That figure already includes charging losses and the winter penalty of heating the cabin and battery.

To price a single appliance-style load, such as a Level 1 charger plugged into a wall socket, the electricity cost calculator takes watts and hours per day. A 1,440 W Level 1 charger running 8 hours a night uses 11.52 kWh, which costs $2.09 at 18.16¢.

Mistakes the calculators cannot catch

  • Using the national average. Residential electricity prices differ by more than a factor of two between states. Read the price per kWh off your bill, including delivery charges, not just the generation line.
  • Ignoring time-of-use rates. Overnight rates can be much lower than the average; charging at peak can be much higher.
  • Mixing up imperial and US gallons. A UK mpg figure is about 20% higher than a US mpg figure for the same car.
  • Assuming the sticker kWh/100 mi in winter. Cold weather raises consumption; use a winter month's data if you drive mostly in winter.

What this guide does not cover

Purchase price, tax credits, insurance, maintenance, depreciation and battery replacement, which usually matter more than energy over a car's life; charger installation cost; emissions comparisons; and prices outside the US. Public charging prices in the tables are illustrative examples, not measured averages.
