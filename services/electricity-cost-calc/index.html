Figuring out what an appliance really costs to run

Utility bills lump every device in the house into one number, so it's hard to tell whether the AC, the old fridge, or a space heater is the real culprit. This electricity cost calculator isolates one appliance at a time: enter its wattage, how many hours a day it runs, and your price per kWh, and it turns that into a cost per day, month, and year — plus the raw kWh consumed, in case your utility bills you in usage tiers rather than a flat rate.

The formula behind the numbers

Three steps, done automatically for every appliance you add:

  • kWh per day = (watts × hours used per day) ÷ 1,000
  • Cost per day = kWh per day × price per kWh
  • Monthly and yearly cost = daily cost × 30.44 (the average days per month) or × 365

If your appliance's nameplate lists kilowatts (kW) instead of watts, switch the unit next to the number field — the calculator converts internally, so the result is identical either way. Quantity lets you model several identical devices (a bank of LED bulbs, for example) without adding separate rows.

Example 1 — a refrigerator. 150W, running 24 hours a day, at $0.15/kWh: 150 × 24 ÷ 1,000 = 3.6 kWh/day. Cost: 3.6 × 0.15 = $0.54/day, about $16.43/month, and $197.10/year.

Example 2 — a window AC unit. 1,500W for 8 hours a day at $0.15/kWh: 1,500 × 8 ÷ 1,000 = 12 kWh/day, or $1.80/day — about $54.75/month just for that one appliance during cooling season.

Example 3 — two LED bulbs. 9W each, quantity 2, 5 hours a day: (9 × 2) × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 0.09 kWh/day — under a cent a day at typical rates, which is why swapping incandescent bulbs for LEDs barely shows up on the bill.

Why this won't match your bill to the cent

  • Tiered / time-of-use rates: many utilities charge more per kWh once you cross a monthly threshold, or charge peak vs. off-peak prices. This tool assumes one flat rate.
  • Fixed fees and taxes: a base service charge and local taxes appear on the bill regardless of usage, and aren't part of a per-appliance estimate.
  • Standby ("phantom") power: devices left plugged in but switched off — chargers, game consoles, set-top boxes — draw a small trickle of power around the clock that adds up across a household.
  • Appliances not entered: the total only reflects what you've added — a whole-house bill also includes lighting, small electronics, and anything you forgot to list.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the electricity cost of an appliance?

Multiply the appliance's power in watts by the hours it runs per day, divide by 1,000 to get kilowatt-hours (kWh), then multiply by your price per kWh. For example, a 150W fridge running 24 hours a day uses 150 × 24 ÷ 1,000 = 3.6 kWh per day. At $0.15 per kWh that's 3.6 × 0.15 = $0.54 a day, about $16.43 a month (using an average of 30.44 days per month), and $197.10 a year. This electricity cost calculator does that math for you and adds up every appliance you enter.

What's the difference between W and kW, and does it matter which I pick?

Watts (W) and kilowatts (kW) both measure power — 1 kW equals 1,000 W. Small appliances like a bulb or a phone charger are usually rated in watts, while larger ones like an oven, water heater, or central air conditioner are often listed in kilowatts on the nameplate or spec sheet. Pick whichever unit matches the number on your appliance's label; the calculator converts internally so the kWh and cost results are identical either way.

How much electricity do common appliances like a fridge, AC, or TV use?

The quick-add presets are typical average figures — a refrigerator around 150W running continuously, an air conditioner around 1,500W for about 8 hours, a space heater around 1,500W for 5 hours, a TV around 100W for 5 hours, a desktop PC around 200W for 6 hours, and an LED bulb around 9W for 5 hours. Real appliances vary by size, age, and efficiency rating, so check the nameplate or manual for your exact model and adjust the watts and hours after adding a preset.

Why doesn't my calculated cost match my actual electricity bill?

This calculator gives a close estimate based on the numbers you enter, but real bills include things this tool can't see: tiered or time-of-use rates that charge more per kWh above a threshold or during peak hours, fixed monthly service fees, taxes, and standby ('phantom') power drawn by devices that are plugged in but off. If your bill runs higher than expected, those extras — plus any appliances you didn't enter — are usually why.

Is my data sent anywhere when I use this calculator?

No. Every calculation happens locally in your browser — the wattage, hours, price, and currency you enter are never uploaded or sent to a server. Your latest inputs are saved only in your browser's local storage so they're there next time you open the page, and you can clear them anytime by clearing your browser data.
