How much protein does your body actually need?

Protein repairs and builds muscle tissue, keeps you full for longer than carbs or fat, and supplies the raw material for enzymes, hormones, antibodies, and hair and skin. The RDA of about 0.8 g/kg body weight is the floor that prevents deficiency in a mostly sedentary adult — it is not a target for anyone who trains, is trying to build or preserve muscle, or is eating fewer calories than they burn. Sports-nutrition research generally puts the useful range for active people between 1.2 and 2.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, with the higher end reserved for resistance training and calorie-restricted "cutting" phases where extra protein helps protect lean mass.

How this calculator works

Enter your body weight in kilograms or pounds, then pick the activity level or goal that best matches you. Each option maps to a gram-per-kilogram range drawn from general sports-nutrition guidance; the calculator multiplies that range by your weight to produce a daily gram target, divides it by your chosen number of meals for a per-meal figure, and converts the midpoint into rough servings of three common protein foods so the number feels concrete rather than abstract.

Worked example 1 — 70 kg, building muscle

At 1.6–2.2 g/kg, a 70 kg person's range is 70 × 1.6 = 112 g to 70 × 2.2 = 154 g per day. Across 4 meals that's about 28–39 g per meal. The midpoint, roughly 133 g, is approximately 4.5 chicken-breast servings (100 g cooked each), 22 large eggs, or 16.5 servings of firm tofu (100 g each) — most people combine a few sources rather than relying on just one.

Worked example 2 — 55 kg, sedentary

At 0.8–1.0 g/kg, a 55 kg person's range is 55 × 0.8 = 44 g to 55 × 1.0 = 55 g per day — close to the RDA floor. Across the default 3 meals that's about 15–18 g per meal, easily covered by a couple of eggs at breakfast and a modest portion of meat, fish, or tofu later in the day.

About the food equivalents

The chicken breast, egg and tofu figures are rounded reference values, not a specific product's nutrition label: about 31 g of protein per 100 g of cooked chicken breast, about 6 g per large egg, and about 8 g per 100 g of firm tofu. Actual protein content varies by cut, brand, and cooking method, so treat these as a quick sanity check — "roughly five chicken breasts' worth of protein" — rather than a precise meal plan.

Frequently asked questions

How much protein do I need per day?

Most general sports-nutrition guidance sets daily protein need as a range per kilogram of body weight rather than one fixed number, because needs scale with activity and goal. Sedentary adults typically do well around 0.8–1.0 g/kg (close to the RDA), regularly active people around 1.2–1.6 g/kg, and those building muscle or losing weight while preserving lean mass often aim for 1.6–2.2 g/kg. This calculator multiplies your body weight by the appropriate range for the activity level or goal you select, so you get a personalized gram target instead of a single generic number.

Worked example: protein intake for a 70 kg (154 lb) person building muscle

For a 70 kg person with a muscle-gain goal (1.6–2.2 g/kg), the range is 70 × 1.6 = 112 g to 70 × 2.2 = 154 g of protein per day. Split across 4 meals that's roughly 28–39 g per meal. At the midpoint of about 133 g, that's approximately equal to 4.5 chicken-breast servings (100 g cooked each), 22 large eggs, or 16.5 servings of firm tofu (100 g each) — in practice most people mix protein sources across meals rather than eating just one.

Is eating more protein always better, and is there a safe upper limit?

No — more isn't automatically better. For healthy adults without kidney disease, intakes up to roughly 2.2 g/kg body weight per day are generally considered safe and already sit at the high end of what research shows adds a training benefit; going well beyond that mainly displaces other nutrients rather than building more muscle. This calculator caps its guideline range at 2.2 g/kg for that reason. People with kidney disease, liver disease, or other conditions affecting protein metabolism should follow a lower target set by their doctor or dietitian rather than a general calculator.

Does this protein calculator send my weight or any data to a server?

No. Your body weight, unit choice, activity level and meal count are processed entirely in your browser using JavaScript — nothing is uploaded, logged, or stored on a server. The only place your inputs are saved is your own browser's localStorage, purely so the form remembers your last entry the next time you open the page, and you can clear that at any time by clearing your browser data.

No — this tool provides general educational estimates based on widely cited sports-nutrition guidance (protein ranges roughly 0.8–2.2 g/kg body weight depending on activity and goal), not a personalized medical or dietary recommendation. It cannot account for your full health history, medications, kidney or liver function, pregnancy, or other individual factors. For a plan tailored to you, talk to a registered dietitian or your physician, especially if you have an existing medical condition.
