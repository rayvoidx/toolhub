How this debt payoff calculator works

Paying off a credit card, personal loan, or any revolving balance comes down to a tug-of-war between two numbers every month: the interest that accrues on what you still owe, and the part of your payment left over to actually shrink that balance. This calculator runs that tug-of-war month by month until the balance reaches zero, so you can see exactly how many months it takes, how much interest you'll pay in total, and the calendar date you'll be debt-free — without opening a spreadsheet.

The math behind the numbers

Every month follows the same two steps:

  • Interest charged = remaining balance × (APR ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
  • Principal paid down = payment − interest charged

The next month starts from the new, smaller balance, and the cycle repeats until nothing is left. Because the interest portion shrinks a little each month, the principal portion grows a little each month even though your payment stays the same — which is why payoff schedules speed up near the end.

Example 1 — a typical card balance. Balance $5,000, APR 19.99%, payment $150/month. It takes 50 months (4 years 2 months) to reach zero, and the total interest paid along the way is $2,357.06 — almost half of the original balance.

Example 2 — the same balance, $50 extra a month. Bump the payment to $200/month (the same $150 plus $50 extra) and the payoff drops to 33 months with only $1,521.02 in interest — a saving of $836.04 and 17 months for $50 more each month. Extra payments go straight to principal, so they're one of the highest-leverage moves in personal finance.

Example 3 — target-date mode. Same $5,000 balance at 19.99% APR, but instead of picking a payment you pick a date: pay it off in 24 months and the required payment works out to about $254.45/month, with total interest of roughly $1,106.91 — noticeably less than the drawn-out minimum-payment path.

The "never pays off" guard

Revolving debt has a hard floor: if your payment is less than or equal to the interest charged that month, the balance cannot go down — it stays exactly the same or grows, no matter how many months pass. A $5,000 balance at 24% APR accrues exactly $100.00 in interest every single month; a $90 payment can never make a dent in it. This calculator checks for that condition before running any schedule and, instead of silently producing a meaningless multi-century payoff time, tells you plainly that the payment will never pay off the balance and shows the minimum viable payment — the smallest amount above that month's interest that lets the balance finally start moving downward.

Two ways to use this calculator

By monthly payment starts from what you can actually afford each month and tells you how long it will take and what it will cost in interest. By target payoff date flips the question around: pick the date you want to be debt-free, and the calculator works backward to the fixed monthly payment required to hit it. This second mode is especially useful for comparing against a card issuer's minimum payment, which is usually set low on purpose (often 1–3% of the balance) so that interest keeps compounding for years.

Reading the payoff schedule

The full schedule breaks every period into payment, interest, principal, and remaining balance, toggled between a yearly summary (useful for longer payoffs) and a full monthly breakdown (useful for double-checking a specific month). Because interest is always calculated on the balance that's left, the interest column shrinks and the principal column grows every single period — the schedule makes that shift visible instead of leaving it as an abstract idea.

Frequently asked questions

How is the number of months to pay off debt calculated?

Each month the calculator charges interest on whatever balance is left — balance × (APR ÷ 12 ÷ 100) — then applies the rest of your payment to the principal, and repeats until the balance hits zero. For example, a $5,000 balance at 19.99% APR with a $150 monthly payment takes 50 months (4 years 2 months) to clear and costs $2,357.06 in total interest — almost half the original balance, which is exactly what happens when payments stay close to a card's minimum.

What happens if my payment doesn't cover the monthly interest?

If your payment is less than or equal to that month's interest charge, the balance never shrinks — every dollar you pay is absorbed by interest before any of it reaches the principal, so the debt sits still or even grows. For example, a $5,000 balance at 24% APR accrues exactly $100.00 in interest every month; a $90 payment can never make progress. This calculator flags that as "will never pay off" and shows the minimum viable payment (about $100.02 here) — the smallest amount that lets the balance start moving down at all, though a real payoff plan needs more room than that bare minimum.

How much does paying extra every month actually save?

Extra payments skip interest entirely and go straight to principal, so they save more than they look like at first glance. On the same $5,000 balance at 19.99% APR, a $150 monthly payment takes 50 months and $2,357.06 in interest; adding $50 extra (making it $200 total) pays it off in 33 months for $1,521.02 in interest — a saving of $836.04 and 17 months for $50 more a month.

Should I use my card's minimum payment or the target-date mode?

Card issuers usually set the minimum payment low on purpose — often 1–3% of the balance — so the debt lingers for years and interest keeps compounding. Target-date mode flips the question: pick the date you want to be debt-free by, and the calculator works out the fixed monthly payment that gets you there. Wiping out that same $5,000 balance in 24 months at 19.99% APR takes about $254.45 a month and costs roughly $1,106.91 in interest, instead of a shrinking minimum payment that drags on for years.

Is my balance or payment information sent to a server?

No. This calculator runs entirely in your browser — your balance, APR, payment, and every result are computed locally and never uploaded or stored on a server. There's no sign-up and no tracking of your numbers, so you can plan a payoff strategy for a private debt with peace of mind.
