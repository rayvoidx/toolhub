    How long a nap should be, and why the length matters more than the hour

    A nap is not a small night's sleep. It is a ride through the first part of a sleep cycle, and where the alarm catches you decides whether you wake up sharp or wrecked. The cycle runs roughly like this: stage 1 (drifting, 1 to 5 minutes), stage 2 (light but real sleep, about 20 minutes), stage 3 (slow-wave or deep sleep, roughly minutes 25 to 60), then REM near the 70 to 90 minute mark. Wake in stage 1 or 2 and you feel fine within a minute. Wake in stage 3 and you get sleep inertia — the heavy, slightly stupid feeling that can last 15 to 30 minutes.

    That is the whole design of this calculator. You give it the time you are lying down and how long you usually take to fall asleep; it adds the sleep length on top of the moment you actually fall asleep, not the moment your head hits the pillow. Ten minutes of staring at the ceiling is the difference between a 20-minute nap and a 30-minute one, and 30 minutes is right at the edge of deep sleep.

    Worked example: the 2pm office nap

    You have a meeting at 3pm and it is 1:45pm. You pick the power nap (20 minutes) and the typical 10-minute fall-asleep buffer. The tool adds 10 minutes to 1:45pm, so you are actually asleep at about 1:55pm, and 20 minutes of sleep puts the alarm at 2:15pm. Total time away from the desk: 30 minutes. You are back at your keyboard by 2:20pm with fifteen minutes to spare and no grogginess to shake off before the meeting.

    Change one input and see how it moves. Say you know you take 15 minutes to drop off — the same 1:45pm start now means the alarm should be at 2:20pm, not 2:15pm. People who set the alarm for "twenty minutes from now" are usually giving themselves about ten minutes of real sleep, then wondering why the nap did nothing.

    Worked example: the 90-minute weekend nap

    It is Saturday, you are running a sleep debt from the week, and you have a genuinely free block. Lie down at 1:30pm with a 10-minute buffer: asleep around 1:40pm, one full cycle through deep sleep and REM, alarm at 3:10pm. You end the cycle back in light sleep, which is why 90 minutes wakes cleanly even though it is more than four times the length of a power nap. REM is also where the memory consolidation happens that a 20-minute nap does not reach.

    Why 60 minutes is the worst choice

    The alternatives list flags the 60-minute option for a reason. An hour lands you deep in slow-wave sleep, the stage with the highest arousal threshold. Waking there triggers the strongest sleep inertia, and studies of shift workers have measured reaction times immediately after such a nap that are worse than before it. The benefit is still there underneath — it arrives after the fog clears — but if you need to be useful in the next half hour, an hour is the one length to avoid. Either cut it to 20 to 26 minutes or extend it to 90.

    The NASA 26-minute nap

    The famous number comes from a NASA and FAA study of long-haul pilots: a planned 26-minute cockpit nap improved performance by 34 percent and alertness by 54 percent compared with the control group. Twenty-six minutes is a compromise — long enough to bank real stage 2 sleep, short enough that most sleepers have not yet sunk far into slow-wave sleep. It is the option to pick when 20 minutes never feels like enough.

    The coffee nap

    Adenosine builds up in the brain across the day and is what makes you feel sleepy. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors, and sleep clears adenosine itself. Drink an espresso quickly, nap 20 minutes, and the two mechanisms line up: caffeine reaches peak plasma concentration at roughly 20 to 30 minutes, arriving exactly as you wake into a brain that has already dumped some of its adenosine load. Small studies on simulated driving found the combination beat either intervention alone.

    Shift workers and night workers

    If you work nights, the 1 to 3pm advice does not apply to you — your circadian dip has moved. The useful rule is the structural one: nap before the shift rather than during the deepest part of it if you can, keep it to 20 minutes when you must be operational immediately afterwards, and if you have a two-hour break, take the full 90 and allow 20 minutes of recovery before doing anything safety-critical. Set the start time in this tool to your planned lie-down time, whatever the clock says.

    Limits of this calculator

    Sleep stage timings are population averages. Your own cycle may run 80 or 100 minutes rather than 90, sleep debt makes you drop into deep sleep much faster than usual, and alcohol, medication and age all shift the picture. If you are chronically exhausted, a 20-minute nap can go straight to slow-wave sleep and still leave you groggy — that is a signal about your night sleep, not a fault in the arithmetic. This tool covers daytime naps; for bedtime and wake-up planning across a whole night, use a sleep cycle calculator instead.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long should a nap be?

    For a quick recharge, 20 minutes of actual sleep is the sweet spot: you stay in light stages 1 and 2, so waking is easy and alertness picks up within minutes. If you have a full 90 minutes, that covers one complete sleep cycle and ends back near light sleep. The lengths to avoid are roughly 45 to 75 minutes, which tend to wake you mid deep sleep.

    When is the best time of day to nap?

    Most people hit a circadian dip between about 1pm and 3pm, which is when a nap comes easiest and costs the least. Napping after 3pm starts eating into the sleep pressure that helps you fall asleep at night. A good rule is to be awake from the nap at least six hours before your usual bedtime.

    Why do I wake up groggy from a 60-minute nap?

    Around 30 to 60 minutes in, you are usually in slow-wave (deep) sleep. An alarm there produces sleep inertia — the heavy, confused feeling that can last 15 to 30 minutes and briefly leaves you worse off than no nap at all. Either stop at 20 to 26 minutes or ride the full 90-minute cycle to its end.

    Does the coffee nap really work?

    Drink an espresso or strong coffee quickly, then nap 20 minutes. Caffeine needs about 20 to 30 minutes to reach peak effect, so it arrives just as you wake, and the nap has already cleared some of the adenosine that caffeine competes with. Small driving-alertness studies found the combination beat either the coffee or the nap alone.
