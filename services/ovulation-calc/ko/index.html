What this calculator estimates

This tool estimates the day you are most likely to ovulate, and the fertile window around it, from the calendar of your menstrual cycle. Ovulation is the point when an ovary releases an egg; conception is only possible in a short window around it. Knowing roughly when that window falls can help with planning a pregnancy and with understanding your own cycle — as long as you keep the important caveats below in mind.

How to use it

  1. First day of your last period. Enter the first day of actual bleeding, which counts as day 1 of the cycle — not the day bleeding stopped.
  2. Average cycle length. Count from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. Most cycles run 21 to 45 days; 28 is a textbook average, not a rule.

The rule behind the estimate

The calculation rests on one reasonably stable fact: the luteal phase — the stretch between ovulation and the next period — lasts close to 14 days for most people, even when the cycle as a whole is long or short. So ovulation is estimated by counting back 14 days from the expected next period, which is the same as cycle length minus 14, measured from the start of the cycle.

The fertile window is then the ovulation day plus the five days before it. That length is not arbitrary: sperm can survive in the reproductive tract for up to about five days, while the egg itself lives only 12 to 24 hours, so the days leading up to ovulation matter as much as the day itself.

Example 1 — a 28-day cycle. If your last period started on March 1 with a 28-day cycle, ovulation is estimated on day 28 − 14 = day 14, which is March 14. The fertile window runs day 10 to day 14 (March 10–14), and the next period is expected around March 29.

Example 2 — a longer cycle. With a last period of June 1 and a 32-day cycle, ovulation moves to day 32 − 14 = day 18, June 18, with a fertile window of June 14–18. Assuming "day 14" here would have been four days too early.

Example 3 — a short cycle. On a 24-day cycle, ovulation is estimated on day 24 − 14 = day 10 — far from the day 14 many people assume. That is why the same calendar date can be fertile for one person and not for another.

Where the estimate breaks down

  • Irregular cycles. If your cycle length varies month to month, the ovulation day shifts with it, and a single calendar prediction can be off by several days.
  • The day-14 myth. Ovulation on day 14 is typical only for a 28-day cycle. Assuming it for every cycle is the most common mistake.
  • Life happens. Stress, illness, travel, and some medications can delay or suppress ovulation in a given month, and the calendar cannot see any of that.

Important limits

This is a calendar estimate, not a medical test and not a form of contraception. Calendar-based methods have high real-world failure rates because ovulation timing genuinely varies. To confirm ovulation, to track a possible pregnancy, or for any decision about contraception or fertility, use ovulation tests or speak with a doctor or midwife. Frequently asked questions

How is the ovulation day calculated?

This ovulation calculator uses the standard luteal-phase method: your next period date (last period + cycle length) minus 14 days. The luteal phase — from ovulation to the next period — is the most stable part of the menstrual cycle at about 14 days, while the first half varies. So with a 28-day cycle ovulation lands near day 14, and with a 32-day cycle it is closer to day 18.

When exactly is the fertile window?

From 5 days before ovulation to 1 day after — a 7-day window. Sperm can survive up to about 5 days in fertile cervical mucus, and the egg lives roughly 12–24 hours after release, so the days just before ovulation matter most. This fertile window calculator marks all 7 days, but the highest-chance days are the two or three before ovulation plus the day itself.

Can I use this as birth control?

No. Calendar-based (rhythm) methods have a high failure rate — with typical use, roughly 12–24 of every 100 women get pregnant within a year — because ovulation shifts with stress, illness, travel and sleep. This fertility calculator is for planning and awareness only. For contraception, ask a doctor about a proven method.

What if my menstrual cycle is irregular?

Use your average length over the last 3–6 cycles and treat the result as a rough guide. If your cycles vary by more than a few days, or fall outside 21–45 days, a period predictor alone cannot pin down ovulation — ovulation test kits (LH strips), basal body temperature or cervical mucus tracking are more reliable. Ongoing irregularity is worth raising with a doctor.

Is my period data sent to a server?

No. Every date is calculated in your browser with JavaScript. Your last period date and cycle length are saved only in this browser's local storage so the form is ready next time, and "Clear saved dates" wipes them immediately. There is no account, no upload and no tracking of what you enter.
