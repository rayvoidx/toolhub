Why cup measurements need the ingredient named

A cup is a unit of volume — the space something occupies. Grams measure mass. The bridge between them is density, and density changes completely from one ingredient to the next. A US cup holds 236.6 ml of anything, but filling it with flour gives roughly 125 g while the same cup of granulated sugar gives about 200 g. That is a 60 percent difference from one identical scoop, which is why a converter that ignores the ingredient will quietly ruin a recipe.

Baking is where this matters most. Bread and cake formulas depend on ratios of flour to liquid to fat; being 25 g out on flour changes hydration enough to shift crumb and rise. Cooking is more forgiving — a little extra rice or oil rarely breaks a dish — but the arithmetic is the same either way.

Worked example 1: a cake recipe in cups

The recipe asks for 2 cups all-purpose flour, 1 cup granulated sugar, and 1/2 cup butter. Converted: flour 2 x 125 = 250 g, sugar 1 x 200 = 200 g, butter 0.5 x 227 = 113 g. Sugar and butter sit close together in weight per cup while flour is far lighter — the light, airy one is exactly the ingredient most likely to be mis-measured by scooping.

Worked example 2: spoons for small quantities

One tablespoon is 14.79 ml. For honey, at roughly 1.42 g per ml, that is about 21 g — noticeably heavier than a tablespoon of water at 14.8 g. Sticky, dense ingredients like honey, molasses and syrup are where volume-to-weight surprises people most, and also where a scale beats a spoon you then have to scrape clean.

Worked example 3: US cup versus metric cup

An Australian recipe calling for 1 metric cup of rice means 250 ml, which at 0.80 g/ml is about 201 g. Reading it as a US cup gives 236.6 ml, or 190 g — about 5 percent short. One cup of rice will not ruin dinner, but across a five-ingredient bake the errors accumulate in the same direction and the dough tells on you.

How to measure so the numbers hold

For flour and other powders, spoon the ingredient into the cup and level the top with a straight edge. Dipping the cup into the bag compacts the flour and can add 20 to 30 g per cup — the single largest source of error in home baking. Brown sugar is the exception: recipes assume it is packed down firmly, which is why its density is so much higher than granulated sugar.

Butter is easier than it looks. A standard US stick is 113 g, or half a cup, and the wrapper is usually marked in tablespoons. Liquids should be read at eye level in a clear jug rather than a dry-measure cup, since the meniscus makes a surprising difference at small volumes.

None of this is needed if you own a kitchen scale. Weighing is faster, uses fewer dishes, and removes packing technique from the equation entirely. Use this converter to translate a cup-based recipe into grams once, write the grams in the margin, and you never have to think about it again.

Frequently asked questions

Why is one cup of flour not the same weight as one cup of sugar?

A cup measures volume, not weight, and ingredients have different densities. A cup of all-purpose flour is about 125 g, while a cup of granulated sugar is about 200 g. Converting without naming the ingredient is guesswork, which is why this tool asks which one you are measuring.

Is a US cup the same as a metric cup?

No. A US cup is 236.6 ml, a metric cup (Australia, New Zealand) is 250 ml, and a UK recipe usually gives weights instead. This converter defaults to the US cup, so a metric-cup recipe will read about 6 percent light unless you pick the metric option.

How much does scooping technique change the result?

For flour, a lot. Scooping straight from the bag compacts it and can add 20 to 30 g per cup versus spooning it in and levelling off. If a recipe gives grams, use the scale — it removes the entire problem.
