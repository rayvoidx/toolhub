Recipe Scaler

Half a recipe, double it, or stretch it to seven people — fractions handled for you.

Scale factor: —

Quantities scale by the factor; salt, spices, leavening and cooking time do not. Read the notes below before you triple a cake.

Scaling a recipe, step by step

Every scaled recipe starts with one number: the factor. Divide the servings you want by the servings the recipe makes. A recipe for 4 that has to feed 6 gives 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5, so every quantity is multiplied by 1.5. Halving is the same arithmetic in reverse — 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5. The hard part was never the multiplication; it is that kitchens do not own a 0.75 teaspoon, so the answer has to come back as a fraction you can actually measure.

Worked example: 4 servings to 6

Take the shopping list this tool starts with: 2 cups flour, 1/2 tsp salt, 3 tbsp butter, 1 egg. At a factor of 1.5 the flour becomes 3 cups, the salt becomes 3/4 tsp, the butter becomes 4 1/2 tbsp, and the egg becomes 1 1/2. Three of those are easy. The half egg is the one everybody gets stuck on: crack two eggs, beat the second one smooth, and add half of it by weight — a large US egg is about 50 g without the shell, so half is 25 g, or roughly 1 1/2 tablespoons. The tool flags that line for you rather than pretending 1.5 eggs is a normal instruction.

The salt deserves a second look too. Multiplied out it reads 3/4 tsp, but seasoning is the one ingredient most cooks should hold back on when scaling up. Start at about 75% of the scaled figure — a scant 1/2 teaspoon here — cook, taste, and add the rest at the end. You can always put salt in; you cannot take it out.

Worked example: halving a tray bake

A tray bake for 12 that you want for 5 gives a factor of 5 ÷ 12 = 0.417. A cup of sugar becomes 0.417 cups, which the tool rounds to the nearest usable measure: 3/8 cup, or 6 tablespoons if your set stops at quarters. 350 g flour becomes 146 g — and this is where a scale earns its price, because 0.417 of a cup measured by scooping is a coin toss. Two teaspoons of baking powder become a shade under 1 teaspoon, which is fine; leavening only misbehaves in the other direction.

What does not scale in a straight line

Ingredient or stepRule of thumb
Salt and spicesStart at ~75% of the scaled amount, correct at the end
Chilli, garlic, strong herbsSame 75% rule — intensity grows faster than volume
Baking powder, soda, yeastFull amount up to 2x; ~80% at 3x and beyond
Alcohol, vanilla, extractsScale at ~80% when going up; they concentrate as liquid reduces
Cooking timeBarely changes. Depth of the pan drives it, not total volume
Oven temperatureNever scales. Same temperature, longer bake for a deeper tin
Pan or pot sizeScale the area, not the diameter (see below)
Fat for fryingEnough to coat the pan — not a multiple of the original

Cooking time is the rule people break most often. Doubling a beef stew does not double two hours into four. The pot is wider, the heat still has to travel through the same depth of liquid, and the extra mass mostly costs you a longer come-up to a simmer — usually 10 to 20 minutes more, then the same braise. Roasts are the exception worth knowing: two chickens in one oven take a bit longer than one, because the oven has to reheat more mass, but nothing like twice as long.

Pan maths: match the area

Cake tins are labelled by diameter and filled by area, which is why a "one inch bigger" tin swallows a batter that used to fill it. Area of a round tin is pi times the radius squared. An 8-inch round is 3.14 x 4² = 50.3 square inches; a 9-inch round is 3.14 x 4.5² = 63.6. The ratio is 63.6 ÷ 50.3 = 1.27, so going from an 8-inch to a 9-inch tin means scaling the recipe by about 1.27 to keep the same depth — or accepting a flatter, faster-baking cake if you do not.

Rectangular tins are easier: length times width. An 8x8 is 64 square inches, a 9x13 is 117, so the bigger tin takes about 1.8 times the batter. Two 8-inch rounds equal roughly one 9x13 (100.5 vs 117 square inches, close enough for most bakes). Whatever the shape, keep the batter depth between about half and two-thirds of the tin height, and add 5 to 15 minutes if the scaled batter sits deeper than the original.

Weigh it if you are scaling up

Volume measures have a tolerance that compounds. A cup of flour is anywhere from 120 g to 150 g depending on whether it was scooped or spooned, so a recipe tripled by cup ends up somewhere in a 90 g range — the difference between a tender crumb and a brick. Grams do not have that problem. If you plan to scale often, switch to a digital scale for flour, sugar and butter and leave the cups for liquids. It is the single change that makes a scaled bake behave like the original.

Limits of this tool

It reads a number at the start of each line and multiplies it. Lines with no leading quantity — section headings, "salt to taste", "a splash of oil" — are passed through untouched and marked, so nothing quietly vanishes from your list. It does not know that 16 tablespoons make a cup, so an oversized result like 12 tbsp stays 12 tbsp rather than becoming 3/4 cup. It does not adjust anything for altitude, pan material or convection ovens, and it cannot tell you whether your mixer can handle the bowl volume you are about to demand of it. For everything the arithmetic cannot see, the table above is the checklist.

Frequently asked questions

What does not scale linearly in a recipe?

Salt, spices and anything strongly flavoured should start at about 75% of the scaled amount and be adjusted at the end, because the palate reads seasoning on a curve rather than a straight line. Leavening drifts once you pass roughly 3x, so cut baking powder, baking soda and yeast to about 80% of the scaled figure at that point. Cooking time barely scales at all: doubling a stew adds minutes, not double the hours, because heat travels through the depth of the pan rather than the volume of food.

Can I double a baking recipe?

Baking is chemistry, so doubling only works if the ratios stay exact — weigh in grams rather than scooping cups, since a doubled cup of flour can easily be 40 g out. The pan changes too: two 8-inch layers become four 8-inch layers, not two deeper ones, or the centre never sets. Past 3x, mix in batches, as a home mixer cannot aerate a quadrupled batter evenly.

How do I convert pan sizes when scaling?

Match area, not diameter. An 8-inch round pan is about 50 square inches and a 9-inch is about 64, so the larger pan holds roughly 1.27 times as much batter at the same depth — that ratio is your scale factor. For rectangular tins multiply length by width: a 9x13 (117 sq in) takes about 1.8 times what an 8x8 (64 sq in) takes.

How do I halve an egg?

Crack the egg, beat it until smooth, then weigh or measure half of it — a large egg is roughly 50 g out of the shell, so half is 25 g or about 1.5 tablespoons. This tool flags any fractional egg for exactly that reason. A third of an egg works the same way at around 1 tablespoon, and the remainder keeps a day in the fridge, covered.
