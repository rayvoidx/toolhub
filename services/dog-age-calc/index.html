Why "multiply by seven" gets your dog's age wrong

The seven-to-one rule looks like it started as marketing arithmetic: humans lived to about 70, dogs to about 10, so someone divided the two and printed it on a flea-collar box. It is memorable and wrong at both ends of a dog's life. A one-year-old dog is already sexually mature and fully grown in most breeds, behaviourally closer to a teenager than a seven-year-old child. At the other end, a flat multiplier would put a 13-year-old small dog at 91 — older than almost any dog has ever lived — when in reality that small dog is often still an active senior with a couple of good years left.

What actually happens is front-loaded: the first year covers roughly 15 years of human development on its own, the second year adds about nine more, and only after that does ageing settle into a steady yearly increment — one that depends heavily on how big the dog is built to become. This calculator applies that phased curve based on the size class you pick, then shows a second, independent estimate from canine epigenetics as a sanity check on the first.

Getting an accurate reading

  1. Enter the age in years, or switch the unit to months for a puppy under one year — the early months carry more developmental weight than any later year, so precision here matters most.
  2. Pick the adult size class, not the dog's current size. A 4 kg golden retriever puppy is a future large dog, not a small one — sizing by projected adult weight is the single most common input mistake.
  3. Press Convert to see the human-year estimate, a life-stage label (puppy through senior), and the epigenetic cross-check side by side.

The two formulas behind the numbers

Size-phase model: age 1 = 15 human years; age 2 adds 9 more (24 total); each year after that adds a size-dependent step — roughly 4/yr small, 4.5/yr medium, 5.5/yr large, 6.5/yr giant.

Epigenetic model (2020 DNA-methylation study): human age = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31.

The phase model mirrors how veterinary charts are actually built — piecewise, from clinical milestones. The epigenetic formula instead comes from comparing chemical markers on canine and human DNA directly, so it needs no phases or size brackets at all, but the logarithm overshoots badly under one year of age, which is why this tool leaves that estimate blank for puppies.

Worked examples

A 7-year-old Labrador (large, ~30 kg). Years one and two bank 24. The remaining five years add 5.5 each: 5 × 5.5 = 27.5. Total ≈ 52 human years — entering the mature-to-senior transition where most vets add twice-yearly bloodwork.
A 7-year-old Jack Russell (small, ~7 kg). Same first-two-year total of 24, but the remaining five add only 4 each: 20. Total ≈ 44 human years — identical birthday to the Labrador above, eight human years younger, purely from adult size.
A 5-year-old Great Dane (giant). 24 + (3 × 6.5) = 43.5 human years, yet the life-stage label already reads senior, since giant breeds typically enter their final third of life by age six. The number and the stage label are answering different questions.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing by current weight, not adult weight. A large-breed puppy sized as "small" will understate every later year by roughly 1.5 human years per year of age.
  • Reapplying "times seven" after year two. The steady per-year increment is 4 to 6.5, never seven — the flat rule only happens to be close during a narrow window in mid-adulthood for medium dogs.
  • Reading the epigenetic number for a puppy. Below one year the logarithmic formula produces an inflated figure, which is why this tool intentionally leaves it blank rather than show a misleading number.
  • Ignoring the life-stage label. Two dogs can share a human-year estimate yet sit in different life stages — the label, not the raw number, is what should drive vet-visit frequency.

What these numbers don't capture

Both models are population averages. An individual dog's real biological age is shaped by breed-specific health predispositions, neuter status, diet, dental care, and exercise — factors no age-conversion formula can see. The practical use of a human-year estimate is context, not diagnosis: knowing a seven-year-old giant breed is already a senior citizen should change how you think about anaesthesia risk, joint supplements, and screening frequency. As a rough guide, annual senior bloodwork is worth starting around age nine for small dogs, seven for large dogs, and five to six for giants — a vet's hands-on assessment always outranks any number on this page.

Frequently asked questions

Is one dog year really seven human years?

No. Dogs mature fast then slow down: a one-year-old dog is roughly a 15-year-old human, and the second year adds about nine more. After that each year adds four to six human years depending on size, so the multiplier is never a flat seven.

Why does size change the answer?

Large and giant breeds age faster after adulthood. A 7-year-old small dog is around 44 in human terms, while a 7-year-old giant breed is closer to 57. Big dogs reach senior status at 6 to 7 years; small dogs often not until 9 or 10.

What is the epigenetic estimate?

A 2020 study on DNA methylation proposed human age = 16 x ln(dog age) + 31. It matches lifespan biology well for adult dogs and is shown here alongside the size chart. It overshoots for puppies under one year, so the tool leaves it blank there.
