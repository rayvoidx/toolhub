What an ISO 8601 week number actually is

An ISO 8601 week number is a standardized way to count weeks within a year, defined by the international standard ISO 8601. Unlike calendars that simply divide the year into chunks of seven days starting January 1st, ISO weeks always run Monday through Sunday, and the numbering is anchored to whichever week contains the year's first Thursday. This single rule is what makes ISO week numbers consistent worldwide, and it is why the number you see on a European business calendar, a project sprint planner, or a manufacturing date code is usually the ISO week, not a naive day-count.

How the week number is calculated, step by step

Given any date, the ISO week number is found with three steps:

  1. Find the Thursday of that date's own week. Since weeks run Monday–Sunday, every date has exactly one Thursday in its week.
  2. Read the year that Thursday falls in. This is the date's "ISO week-year" — it is usually the same as the calendar year, but not always (more on that below).
  3. Count Monday-to-Sunday weeks from January 1st of that year up to the date. The count you land on is the week number, from 1 to 52 or 53.

Worked example. Take July 30, 2026. That date is a Thursday itself, so no adjustment is needed — it falls in the same week whose Thursday defines it. Counting Monday–Sunday weeks from January 1, 2026 (a Thursday) forward lands on week 31 of 2026, running Monday July 27 through Sunday August 2, 2026.

Worked example. January 4th is always in week 1 of its year by definition, because ISO 8601 explicitly anchors week 1 to the week containing January 4th (equivalent to the week containing the year's first Thursday). In 2027, January 4th is a Monday, so week 1 of 2027 runs January 4–10, 2027 exactly.

52 weeks or 53? Why the count changes

Seven days times 52 weeks is 364 days — one short of a common year (365 days) and two short of a leap year (366 days). That leftover day has to belong to some week, so most years still show 52 complete ISO weeks with the stray day(s) folded into the neighboring year's week 1 or the previous year's final week. A year only grows a 53rd ISO week when its first Thursday lands late enough in the calendar that an extra full week is needed to reach December 31st — concretely, when January 1st falls on a Thursday, or, in a leap year, when January 1st falls on a Wednesday.

The year-boundary edge case, explained with real dates

Because week 1 is tied to the first Thursday rather than to January 1st, a handful of days right around New Year's can carry a week number that "belongs" to a different year than their calendar date suggests:

  • Late December into next year's week 1: December 30 and 31, 2024 both fall in ISO week 1 of 2025, even though the calendar still reads 2024.
  • Early January into last year's final week: January 1, 2023 falls in ISO week 52 of 2022, even though the calendar has already turned to 2023.

Neither case is an error — it is exactly how ISO 8601 is designed to behave, and it is the same reason a year can have 52 or 53 weeks in the first place. The date checker above automatically detects and explains this whenever it applies to the date you enter.

Where ISO week numbers are actually used

ISO week numbers show up more often than most people realize: agile teams plan "sprint week 31," manufacturers stamp week-based date codes on packaging and electronics (a code like "2631" often means week 31 of 2026), payroll and shift schedules in much of Europe are built around week numbers instead of month boundaries, and many desk calendars and calendar apps display a small week-number column down the side. Knowing your current ISO week — and being able to check any date's week in a couple of clicks — makes reading all of these systems straightforward instead of guesswork.

How is the ISO 8601 week number calculated?

ISO 8601 weeks start on Monday and are numbered from 1. Week 1 of a year is defined as the week containing that year's first Thursday, which is equivalent to the week containing January 4th. To find a date's week number: move to the Thursday of its own week, note which year that Thursday falls in (the 'ISO week-year'), then count how many Monday-to-Sunday weeks fit between January 1st of that year and the date. For example, January 4, 2027 is a Monday, so week 1 of 2027 runs January 4-10; a date like July 30, 2026 lands in week 31 of 2026. Type any date into the checker above to see this worked out automatically.

Why do some years have 53 ISO weeks instead of 52?

A year has 365 or 366 days, but 52 weeks only cover 364 days, so the leftover day (or two, in a leap year) has to go somewhere. A calendar year gets a 53rd ISO week whenever January 1st falls on a Thursday, or when it falls on a Wednesday in a leap year — both push the year's first Thursday late enough that an extra full week is needed to reach December 31st. Recent examples include 2020, 2026, and 2032. The full-year table above shows the exact week count for any year you enter, so you never have to work it out by hand.

Why can a date in late December or early January belong to a different ISO week-year than its calendar year?

Because week 1 is anchored to the year's first Thursday rather than to January 1st, the two or three days right before or after New Year's Day can end up in the 'wrong' year's week count. December 29-31 sometimes fall in week 1 of the following year — for example, December 30-31, 2024 are both in ISO week 1 of 2025. Symmetrically, January 1-3 can fall in the last week of the previous year — January 1, 2023 is in ISO week 52 of 2022. The date checker above flags this automatically whenever a date's calendar year and ISO week-year don't match.
