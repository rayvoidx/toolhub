Margin and markup: same profit, different percentage

Every sale produces one dollar figure of profit, but there are two very different ways to express that profit as a percentage. Gross margin divides the profit by what you sold it for (revenue). Markup divides the same profit by what it cost you. Mixing the two up is the single most common pricing mistake — a 50% markup is not a 50% margin; it works out to a 33.33% margin.

The formulas behind the calculator

  • Profit = revenue − cost
  • Gross margin % = profit ÷ revenue × 100
  • Markup % = profit ÷ cost × 100
  • Price from cost + margin = cost ÷ (1 − margin/100)
  • Price from cost + markup = cost × (1 + markup/100)

Enter any two of cost, revenue, margin %, or markup % into the calculator above and it solves the other two, plus the profit, immediately — including the awkward case where you only know margin and markup: since one always determines the other, the tool checks whether your two numbers actually agree instead of pretending to solve for cost and revenue out of thin air.

Example 1 — pricing from a target margin. A product costs $40 to make and you want a 25% gross margin. Price = 40 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = 40 ÷ 0.75 = $53.33. Profit is $13.33, which checks out as a 33.33% markup (13.33 ÷ 40) — not the 25% you might have guessed if you'd simply added the margin to the cost.

Example 2 — checking your numbers both ways. A $40 cost item sells for $60. Profit is $20. Margin = 20 ÷ 60 = 33.33%. Markup = 20 ÷ 40 = 50%. Same $20 profit, two different percentages depending on what you divide it by.

Example 3 — selling at a loss. A $100 cost item is discounted to $80. Profit is −$20, a −25% margin and a −20% markup. Negative numbers like this usually mean the price needs to go up or the cost needs to come down, unless it's a deliberate loss-leader.

Margin vs. markup at common values

Because margin is always measured against the (larger) selling price and markup against the (smaller) cost, markup numbers run higher than margin numbers for the same sale — and the gap widens fast as margin climbs toward 100%:

A 50% margin already equals a 100% markup (doubling your cost), and a 75% margin equals a 300% markup (quadrupling it). Margin can never reach 100% — that would require a $0 cost — but markup has no ceiling at all.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Adding a percentage to cost and expecting that margin: adding 25% to a $40 cost gives $50, but that's only a 20% margin (10 ÷ 50), not 25%. For an exact 25% margin, divide by 0.75 instead (see Example 1).
  • Mixing per-unit and total figures: comparing a per-unit cost against total revenue (or vice versa) produces a meaningless percentage. Keep both numbers on the same basis.
  • Treating margin and markup as interchangeable: a supplier quoting "50% markup" and a retailer targeting "50% margin" are asking for very different prices — always confirm which one is meant before you agree to a number.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between margin and markup?

Margin and markup both measure profit from the same sale, but they divide it by different numbers. Gross margin is profit divided by the selling price (revenue), so it answers "what share of each sale is profit?" Markup is profit divided by cost, so it answers "how much did we add on top of what we paid?" A product that costs $40 and sells for $60 has a $20 profit — that's a 33.33% margin (20/60) but a 50% markup (20/40): the same dollars, two different percentages, and the single most common pricing mix-up.

How do I calculate the selling price from cost and a target margin?

Divide the cost by (1 minus the target margin as a decimal): price = cost / (1 − margin/100). For a 25% gross margin on a $40 cost, price = 40 / (1 − 0.25) = 40 / 0.75 = $53.33. You can't just add 25% to the cost — that gives a 25% markup ($50), which works out to only a 20% margin, not 25%. Enter your cost and target margin % into the calculator above and it solves the exact price.

Can gross margin be negative?

Yes — a negative margin means you're selling below cost and losing money on every unit. A $100 cost sold for $80 gives a −$20 profit, a −25% margin (−20/80), and a −20% markup (−20/100). Retailers sometimes accept a negative margin deliberately on loss-leader items to draw in customers, but if it happens by accident it's a sign the price needs to go up or the cost needs to come down. This calculator handles negative inputs and shows the loss clearly rather than an error.

Is my pricing data sent to a server?

No. This margin calculator runs entirely in your browser — the cost, revenue, and percentages you enter are never uploaded, logged, or sent to any server. There's no sign-up and no tracking of your numbers, so you can price a confidential product or quote without exposing your figures.

Why does the gap between margin and markup grow so much at high percentages?

Because margin is capped at 100% (you can't make more profit than your entire selling price), while markup has no upper limit (you can charge any multiple of your cost). A 50% margin already equals a 100% markup — you're doubling your cost — and a 75% margin equals a 300% markup, quadrupling it. The quick-reference table above shows this curve at common values: the higher the margin, the faster the equivalent markup accelerates away from it.
