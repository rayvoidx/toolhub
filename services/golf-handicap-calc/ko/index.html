    How a golf handicap index is actually calculated

    Since 2020 the World Handicap System has replaced the old national schemes used in the United States, Great Britain and Ireland, Australia and most of the golfing world. One method now applies everywhere, and it is simpler than the folklore suggests: every round you post is converted into a score differential, and your Handicap Index is built from the best of those differentials. This calculator runs that arithmetic for one to eight rounds, which is where most golfers start.

    Step 1 — turn each round into a score differential

    The differential formula is: (Adjusted Gross Score − Course Rating) × 113 ÷ Slope Rating, rounded to one decimal place. Course Rating is what a scratch golfer is expected to shoot from those tees; Slope Rating measures how much harder the course plays for a bogey golfer than for a scratch golfer. Both numbers are printed on the scorecard and on the tee signage — use the values for the exact tees you played, not the championship tees.

    Say you shot an adjusted 88 from tees rated 72.0 with a slope of 130. The differential is (88 − 72.0) × 113 ÷ 130 = 16 × 113 ÷ 130 = 13.9. Shooting the same 88 from an easier set of tees rated 70.2 with a slope of 118 gives (88 − 70.2) × 113 ÷ 118 = 17.0 — a worse differential, because the course gave you less to fight against. That is the entire point of the system: the differential measures the round, not the scorecard.

    Step 2 — the short-record table

    With a full record of 20 scores, the World Handicap System averages your best 8 differentials. With fewer scores it uses a reduced table so a beginner is not stuck without a number. Three scores use the lowest differential minus 2.0; four use the lowest minus 1.0; five use the lowest as it stands; six use the average of the lowest two minus 1.0; seven or eight use the average of the lowest two; and so on up to the full best-8-of-20. This tool covers the one-to-eight band, applying the minus-2.0 adjustment for one or two rounds as well, which is what most club systems do internally while you are still building a record.

    Worked example: three rounds

    Round one: 88 from 72.0 / 130, differential 13.9. Round two: 92 from 71.4 / 124, differential 18.8. Round three: 85 from 70.2 / 118, differential 14.2. The lowest is 13.9, and with three scores you subtract 2.0, giving a Handicap Index of 11.9. Notice that the 85 — the best raw score of the three — is not the round that sets your index. The 88 on a slope-130 course was the better performance.

    Worked example: index to course handicap

    An index is portable; a Course Handicap is the number of strokes you actually receive today. The conversion is Index × (Slope ÷ 113) + (Course Rating − Par), rounded to the nearest whole number. Take the 11.9 index above to a course whose white tees are rated 73.1 with a slope of 138 and a par of 72: 11.9 × 138 ÷ 113 = 14.5, plus (73.1 − 72) = 1.1, giving 15.6, which rounds to 16 strokes. The same index at a gentle municipal track rated 69.4, slope 112, par 71 yields 11.9 × 112 ÷ 113 = 11.8, plus (69.4 − 71) = −1.6, so 10 strokes. Six strokes of difference from one number — this is why you never quote a Course Handicap as your handicap.

    Adjusted gross score, not raw score

    The score you enter is the adjusted gross score, capped hole by hole at net double bogey: par + 2 + any handicap strokes you receive on that hole. On a par 4 where you receive one stroke, the maximum you can post is 7, no matter what actually happened after the tee shot went out of bounds. The cap keeps one disastrous hole from inflating your index, and it is applied before anything else in this calculation. In the United States a score is acceptable if it is played under the Rules of Golf over at least 9 holes with a fellow player; in GB&I, general-play rounds must be registered before you tee off.

    Integrity, and what this tool does not do

    An index only means something if the scores behind it are honest and complete. Posting your good rounds and quietly forgetting the bad ones — sandbagging in reverse — corrupts the number that every match, medal and society day depends on. Post every acceptable round, on the day you play it.

    This calculator is deliberately narrow. It does not apply the Playing Conditions Calculation that adjusts differentials on unusually hard or easy days, nor the soft cap and hard cap that slow a rising index, nor the Low Handicap Index anchor from your last 12 months. It does not combine 9-hole scores, and it will not compute net double bogey for you. Those steps live in your club's software. Use the number here to know roughly where you stand — then post your scores through your club or an authorised provider such as GHIN, and let the official system issue the index you play off.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between a handicap index and a course handicap?

    Your Handicap Index is portable: one number that travels with you to any course. A Course Handicap converts it for the tees you are actually playing — Course Handicap = Index x (Slope / 113) + (Course Rating - Par), rounded to the nearest whole number. A 13.9 index on a 130-slope course rated 72.0 at par 71 plays off 17 strokes.

    Why does the formula divide by 113?

    113 is the slope rating of a course of standard difficulty, the neutral baseline the USGA chose when the Slope system was built. Multiplying by 113 and dividing by the course slope rescales your round onto that standard course. That is what makes a differential from a brutal 148-slope layout comparable with one from a gentle 105-slope track.

    How does the full best-8-of-20 method work?

    Once your record holds 20 acceptable scores, the World Handicap System averages the lowest 8 differentials of those 20 and rounds to one decimal. Each new score pushes the oldest one out, so the index refreshes the day after you post. With fewer than 20 scores a reduced table is used instead, which is exactly what this calculator applies for one to eight rounds.

    What is the net double bogey cap?

    Before a score becomes an adjusted gross score, every hole is capped at net double bogey: par plus two, plus any handicap strokes you receive on that hole. A blow-up 9 on a par 4 where you get one stroke counts as 7. Apply the cap hole by hole first, then enter the adjusted total here.
