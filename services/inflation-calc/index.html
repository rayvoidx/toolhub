What an inflation calculator actually measures

Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises, which means the same unit of currency buys a little less each year. This calculator turns that abstract idea into two concrete answers. In Future value mode, you give it an amount you have today and it tells you what that money will feel like — its purchasing power — after a number of years of compounding inflation, plus the nominal cost you'd need to reach then to match today's buying power. In Past value mode, you flip the direction: give it an amount from some point in the past and it grows that figure forward to today's equivalent, which is the classic "what would this cost today?" question people ask about old prices, wages, or purchases.

How to use this calculator

  1. Choose a calculation direction: Future value (today → later) or Past value (earlier → today).
  2. Enter the amount — today's money in Future value mode, or the historical amount in Past value mode.
  3. Enter the average annual inflation rate you want to assume, or tap one of the quick presets. This is a single steady rate, not a year-by-year historical series.
  4. Enter the number of years, or tap a preset. In Past value mode this is how many years ago the amount is from.
  5. Pick a currency for formatting — the math itself is currency-agnostic.
  6. Read the result cards and expand the year-by-year table to see how the figure moves one year at a time.

The formulas behind the numbers

Future value mode: Purchasing power in N years = Amount ÷ (1 + rate)N. Equivalent cost in N years = Amount × (1 + rate)N.

The first formula shrinks today's amount into what it will really be able to buy later, expressed in today's money. The second grows today's amount into the bigger nominal figure you'd need later just to buy the same things — the two are reciprocals of the same compounding relationship.

Past value mode: Today's equivalent = Past amount × (1 + rate)N.

Prices were lower N years ago, so this formula compounds the historical amount forward by the same rate to land on what an equal amount of purchasing power costs today.

Three worked examples

Example 1 — future purchasing power. $1,000 today, 3% average annual inflation, 10 years. Purchasing power = 1,000 ÷ 1.0310 ≈ $744. Equivalent cost then = 1,000 × 1.0310 ≈ $1,344. In other words, holding that $1,000 as cash for 10 years leaves you able to buy about three-quarters of what you can buy with it today.
Example 2 — what a past price equals today. Something cost $100 back in 2005 (20 years ago from a 2025 reference point), and average annual inflation over that stretch was about 2.8%. Today's equivalent = 100 × 1.02820 ≈ $174 — prices roughly rose 74% over those two decades.
Example 3 — a high-inflation scenario. ₩10,000,000 today at 8% average annual inflation for 5 years: purchasing power = 10,000,000 ÷ 1.085 ≈ ₩6,806,000 — a much sharper erosion than a typical 2–3% target rate, which is exactly why the rate you assume matters so much to the result.

Where the numbers come from — and where they don't

This tool does not ship a database of historical Consumer Price Index (CPI) figures. Real-world inflation is not a single constant rate — it moves up and down year to year, and it differs across product categories (housing and education have often risen faster than electronics, for example). What this calculator gives you is a clean compounding estimate based on one average annual rate that you choose. For an actual historical lookup — "what did $500 in 1990 really cost in 2024, using the real recorded CPI path" — use your country's official calculator (for example, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' CPI Inflation Calculator) or its statistics agency's published index. Use this tool instead for quick projections, planning conversations, and "what if inflation averages X% for Y years" scenarios, where a single assumed rate is exactly what you want to vary and test.

Common mistakes when estimating inflation's effect

  • Treating one year's headline rate as a decades-long average. A single year's inflation print (say, a spike to 8%) rarely holds for 20 or 30 years — long-run planning usually calls for a lower, smoothed average rate instead.
  • Using simple (non-compounding) math. "3% a year for 10 years" is not a 30% total increase — compounding at 3% for 10 years is about 34.4%, and the gap widens with longer periods and higher rates.
  • Forgetting the reciprocal relationship. A 20% cumulative price increase does not mean purchasing power falls by 20% — it falls by 20 ÷ 120 ≈ 16.7%, since the "cost" and "purchasing power" views use the increase differently as a base.
  • Assuming one national average applies evenly. Your personal cost of living may rise faster or slower than the headline number depending on what you actually spend money on.

What this calculator doesn't do

It is not a substitute for official CPI data, and it is not financial, tax, or investment advice — it only performs the arithmetic of compound growth and decay on the amount, rate, and years you supply. Every calculation runs locally in your browser; nothing you type is ever sent to, or stored on, a server.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the future purchasing power of money?

Divide the amount by (1 + inflation rate)^years. For example, $1,000 today at 3% average annual inflation will have the purchasing power of about $744 in 10 years — meaning in 10 years that same $1,000 will only buy what $744 buys today. Switch to Future value mode, enter the amount, rate, and years, and this calculator does the compounding for you, plus the nominal amount you'd need then just to match today's buying power.

How does the "past value" or "what's it worth today" mode work?

Past value mode runs the compounding forward: multiply a past amount by (1 + inflation rate)^years elapsed. For example, if something cost $100 ten years ago and average annual inflation was 3%, its equivalent value today is about $134 — prices rose roughly 34% over that time. Enter the past amount, the rate, and how many years have passed, and switch to Past value mode to see today's equivalent.

Where do I find my country's actual inflation rate?

This calculator does not include a historical CPI database — you supply the average annual rate yourself, so results are only as accurate as the rate you enter. For real, published rates, check your country's statistics agency: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics for CPI, Eurostat for the eurozone's HICP, or your national statistics office. Many publish both the current year-over-year rate and long-run historical averages you can plug into this tool.

Is this the same as an official CPI inflation calculator?

No. Official CPI calculators, like the U.S. BLS calculator, use actual recorded prices for a basket of goods in specific years, so they capture real-world fluctuations year to year. This tool instead assumes one constant average annual rate compounding steadily over the whole period, which is useful for quick projections and "what-if" estimates but will not exactly match historical CPI figures — treat it as an estimate, not official data.

Can I use a negative rate to model deflation, and is my data sent anywhere?

Yes — entering a negative rate models deflation (falling prices), and the calculator handles it the same way, just compounding downward instead of upward. And no, nothing you enter is sent to a server: every calculation, including your last amount, rate, and years, runs and is stored entirely in your browser via localStorage.
