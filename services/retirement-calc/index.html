How this retirement calculator works

This tool projects a nest egg the same way a 401(k) or brokerage projection typically does: it grows whatever you've already saved at your expected annual return for every year remaining until retirement, then layers on every monthly contribution, each one invested at the start of its month and compounded forward the same way (an annuity-due). The two pieces are added together to get your projected balance at retirement, and the calculator also reports how much of that total is money you actually put in versus money the market added on top.

How to use it

  1. Enter your current age and the retirement age you're aiming for — the difference is the number of years your money has to grow.
  2. Enter your current savings (0 is fine if you're starting from scratch) and your planned monthly contribution (0 is fine if you don't plan to add more).
  3. Enter an expected annual return — the presets (5%, 7%, 9%) are common reference points for a diversified portfolio, not guarantees.
  4. Choose Nominal to see raw future dollars, or Real to see the projection in today's purchasing power (enter an expected inflation rate for this mode).
  5. Adjust the safe withdrawal rate if you want something other than the default 4% rule, then press Calculate for your nest egg, safe income estimate, contributions-vs-growth split, and a year-by-year schedule.

The formula behind the projection

Nest egg = (Current savings × growth) + (Monthly contribution × annuity-due factor)

where growth = (1 + monthly rate)months, monthly rate = annual return ÷ 1200, months = years to retirement × 12, and the annuity-due factor = (1 + monthly rate) × (growth − 1) ÷ monthly rate. This is the same compounding math used by dollar-cost-averaging and savings-goal calculators — it simply assumes each month's contribution is invested right away, at the start of the month, rather than at the end.

Three worked examples

Example 1 — starting from scratch. Age 25, retiring at 65 (40 years), $0 saved, $300 a month, 7% expected return, nominal mode. The projected nest egg is about $792,037 — every cent of the roughly $648,000 in growth on top of the $144,000 you actually contributed came from compounding.
Example 2 — a head start plus contributions. Age 30, retiring at 65 (35 years), $50,000 already saved, $500 a month, 7% expected return, nominal mode. The nest egg grows to about $1,481,087 — $260,000 from contributions and roughly $1,221,000 from investment growth, a split the tool shows as a two-color bar.
Example 3 — real (today's dollars) mode. Same inputs as Example 2, but switched to Real mode with 3% expected inflation. The 7% nominal return becomes about a 3.88% real return, and the nest egg comes to roughly $641,349 in today's purchasing power — a more conservative, arguably more useful number for picturing what that retirement balance can actually buy decades from now.

Understanding the 4% rule

The 4% rule comes from historical U.S. market research (commonly traced to the 1998 "Trinity study") that tested how often a portfolio survived 30 years of withdrawals at different starting rates, with the withdrawal amount adjusted for inflation each year after. A 4% starting withdrawal rate held up in the large majority of historical 30-year periods studied. It's a rough planning heuristic, not a guarantee — sequence-of-returns risk, unusually long retirements, and future markets that behave differently from the past can all make a fixed percentage too high or unnecessarily conservative. That's why this calculator lets you adjust the withdrawal rate from 2% to 10% instead of hard-coding 4%.

Common mistakes this calculator helps you avoid

  • Ignoring your own contributions' true weight. Especially over shorter horizons, most of a nest egg is money you put in, not market growth — the contributions-vs-growth split makes that visible instead of hidden inside one lump number.
  • Comparing a nominal projection to today's prices. A nest egg of $1.5 million in 35 years will not buy what $1.5 million buys today; switching to Real mode with a realistic inflation estimate avoids that mismatch.
  • Treating 4% as a law rather than a starting point. Retiring earlier than 30 years of withdrawals, a more conservative outlook, or a strong market history might justify a different rate — try the 3%, 3.5%, and 5% presets to see the income difference.
  • Forgetting that contributions compound too. A monthly contribution invested for decades can outgrow a much larger amount invested for only a few years — the year-by-year schedule shows this curve directly instead of just the final number.

What this calculator doesn't do

It doesn't model taxes, employer matching, Social Security or pension income, sequence-of-returns risk, changing contribution amounts over time, or a portfolio that shifts from stocks to bonds as you approach retirement. It assumes one constant expected return and one constant contribution for the entire period, which is a simplification of how real markets and real careers actually behave. Treat the result as an educational starting estimate for a conversation or your own further planning — not financial, investment, or tax advice.

Frequently asked questions

How does this retirement calculator estimate my nest egg at retirement?

It grows your current savings at your expected annual return for every year left until retirement, then adds every monthly contribution invested at the start of each month and compounded the same way — an annuity-due, the standard way 401(k) and retirement projections are modeled. Worked example: age 30, retiring at 65 (35 years), $50,000 already saved, $500 a month, 7% expected annual return. That grows to about $1,481,087 at retirement, made up of $260,000 from your own contributions and about $1,221,000 from investment growth.

What is the 4% rule, and how is my safe monthly income calculated?

The 4% rule is a rule of thumb from retirement research (the Trinity study) suggesting you can withdraw about 4% of your nest egg in the first year of retirement, then adjust for inflation each year after, with a low historical risk of running out of money over a 30-year retirement. This retirement savings calculator multiplies your projected nest egg by the withdrawal rate (4% by default, adjustable from 2% to 10%) to estimate a safe annual income, then divides by 12 for a monthly figure. On the $1,481,087 example above, a 4% rate gives about $59,243 a year, or roughly $4,937 a month.

What's the difference between the nominal and real (today's dollars) toggle?

Nominal shows the raw future dollar amount your money grows to, using your expected annual return exactly as entered — the number you'd actually see in your account statement decades from now. Real (today's dollars) factors out inflation so the result is expressed in today's purchasing power: it converts your expected return into a real return using (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, and assumes your monthly contribution keeps pace with inflation over time (a common simplifying assumption also used by Coast FIRE-style calculators). Using the same example with 3% inflation, the real return is about 3.88% and the nest egg comes to roughly $641,349 in today's money — smaller than the nominal figure, but more comparable to what you can buy with today's prices.

What if I have no current savings, or don't plan to contribute monthly?

Both are fine — 0 is a valid, explicitly handled value for current savings and for monthly contribution, so you can project a pure monthly-contribution plan starting from zero, or a lump sum with no further contributions. The calculator also requires your retirement age to be greater than your current age, treats a 0% expected return as valid (no growth, just the sum of what you put in), and caps extreme inputs at a very large safety limit rather than producing a broken or infinite result.

Is this financial advice, and is my data sent anywhere?

No to both. This is an educational arithmetic projection based on the numbers and assumed return you enter — it is not financial, investment, or tax advice, and actual market returns, inflation, and your personal circumstances will differ from any fixed assumption. Every calculation runs entirely in your browser: your age, savings, and contribution amounts are never sent to a server, there's no sign-up, and your last entry is saved only in your own browser's local storage.
