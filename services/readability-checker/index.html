What a readability score actually measures

Rudolf Flesch published the Reading Ease formula in 1948 with a deliberately narrow ambition: predict how much effort a passage costs a reader using only two things a clerk could count by hand. Those two things are still all any readability checker uses. The first is average sentence length, words divided by sentences. The second is average word length measured in syllables, syllables divided by words. Everything else about your writing, including whether it is true, kind, organised or interesting, is invisible to the score.

The formula, component by component

Reading Ease = 206.835 - 1.015 x (words per sentence) - 84.6 x (syllables per word). The constant 206.835 is just a starting ceiling. Every extra word per sentence costs about one point, so trimming a 25-word sentence to 15 buys you roughly ten points. Every extra tenth of a syllable per word costs 8.5 points, which is why swapping "utilise" for "use" moves the needle far more than most writers expect. The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level shown next to it, 0.39 x (words per sentence) + 11.8 x (syllables per word) - 15.59, feeds the same two numbers into a US school grade instead of a 0 to 100 scale.

A rewrite, scored at each step

Start with a sentence from a real rejection letter: "In the event that the aforementioned documentation is not received by the department prior to the specified deadline, your application will be subject to administrative closure without further notification." That is 29 words in one sentence with 2.03 syllables per word. Reading Ease lands at 5.3 and the grade level at 19.7 — beyond a doctorate, and a fair description of how it feels to receive.

First pass, split the sentence and cut the padding: "Send your documents before the deadline. If they arrive late, we will close your application without contacting you again." Now it is 19 words across two sentences, 9.5 words per sentence, 1.63 syllables per word. Reading Ease jumps to 59.2 and the grade level drops to 7.4. Nothing was removed except ceremony.

Second pass, prefer short words: "If we do not get your papers by the deadline, we will close your case. We will not write to you again." That is 99.5 on Reading Ease at grade 2.1. Notice this is now arguably too simple for an adult audience — the point of the exercise is that the first rewrite did almost all the useful work. Most writing improves fastest between a score of 20 and 60, then hits diminishing returns.

Who reads comfortably at which score

Above 90 you are in the territory of early readers and safety instructions. The 70 to 79 range is where consumer magazines, popular newspapers and good product pages live; almost any adult reads it without effort. The 60 to 69 band is the plain-English target used by the US Plain Writing Act guidance and by the UK Government Digital Service, which asks teams to write for a reading age of around nine. From 50 to 59 you are in broadsheet feature territory, readable but demanding. Between 30 and 49 sit academic papers and dense B2B copy, and below 30 you find insurance contracts and legal boilerplate. Some US states legislate against that last band: Florida requires life and health insurance policies to score at least 45.

Grade level versus reading ease

The two outputs answer different questions. Reading Ease answers "how hard is this?" on a scale readers can compare across drafts. Grade level answers "how many years of schooling does this assume?" and is easier to explain to a client or a compliance team. In the UK, US grade 8 corresponds roughly to Year 9, and grade 12 to Year 13. If a stakeholder asks for "8th grade reading level", they are asking for a Reading Ease somewhere in the low 60s.

Where the number misleads

Because the formula counts syllables rather than meaning, short jargon is invisible to it. "We will net the spread on the churn cohort" is nine short words and scores beautifully while communicating nothing to an outsider. The reverse also happens: familiar long words such as "information" or "opportunity" drag the score down without troubling a single reader. Syllable counting itself is a spelling heuristic, so names, acronyms and irregular spellings each cost a syllable or two of accuracy.

Sample size matters as well. One sentence gives a number that swings by twenty points if you add a comma clause, which is why this tool refuses to score fewer than ten words. Headings, bullet fragments and code blocks also distort the sentence count, so paste running prose when you want a comparable reading. The practical workflow is simple: score a section, fix the three longest sentences, swap the five heaviest words, then score it again and see whether the change was worth the edit.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good readability score?

For a general audience aim for a Flesch Reading Ease of 60 to 70, which lands near an 8th-grade reading level. Plain-language guidance in the US and UK, including many government and insurance rules, targets that same band. Technical writing for specialists can sit at 30 to 50 without being a problem, but marketing copy below 50 usually loses readers.

How accurate is the syllable count?

The checker counts vowel groups and strips silent endings such as a final 'e' or '-ed', which is how most Flesch tools work without a dictionary. It agrees with a dictionary for the large majority of ordinary English words but slips on names, acronyms and odd spellings like 'queue' or 'business'. Across a paragraph the errors mostly cancel out, so treat a two-point difference between drafts as noise.

Does a high score mean the writing is good?

No. The formula only measures sentence length and word length, and it never reads meaning. You can score 90 with short nonsense sentences and 40 with precise, beautiful prose. Use it as a smoke alarm for long sentences and heavy vocabulary, not as a grade for quality.

How does Flesch compare with SMOG or Gunning Fog?

All three use the same two ingredients, sentence length and word difficulty, but weight them differently. Gunning Fog and SMOG count words of three or more syllables and return a school grade, while Flesch Reading Ease returns a 0 to 100 score where higher is easier. The Flesch-Kincaid grade shown here converts the same inputs into a US school grade, so it usually lands within a grade or two of SMOG.
