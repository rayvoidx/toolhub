Why the break-even point is the first number a new price needs

Every price, cost, or fixed-expense decision eventually reduces to one question: how many units do we have to sell before this stops costing us money? A break-even calculator answers that directly instead of leaving it buried in a spreadsheet. Enter your fixed costs for the period, the price you charge per unit, and the variable cost of producing or delivering one unit, and the tool returns the break-even point in both units and revenue, the contribution margin that drives the whole calculation, and — if you have one — the extra volume needed to hit a specific profit target.

How the numbers are calculated

Everything here comes from one core relationship:

  • Contribution margin per unit = Price per unit − Variable cost per unit
  • Contribution margin ratio = Contribution margin ÷ Price per unit
  • Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per unit
  • Break-even revenue = Break-even units × Price per unit
  • Units for a profit target = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution margin per unit

Because you can't sell a fraction of a physical unit, the headline "break-even units" figure is rounded up to the next whole unit — the exact (fractional) figure is shown alongside it, since it's still useful for planning production runs or batch sizes.

Example 1 — a small product line. Fixed costs $5,000/month, price $25, variable cost $10. Contribution margin = $15 per unit (a 60% margin ratio). Break-even units = 5,000 ÷ 15 = 333.33, rounded up to 334 units since partial units can't be sold. Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ margin ratio = 5,000 ÷ 60% = $8,333.33.

Example 2 — adding a profit target. Same numbers as above, but you want $3,000 profit for the month. Units needed = (5,000 + 3,000) ÷ 15 = 533.33, rounded up to 534 units, requiring about $13,333.33 in revenue — $5,000 more than plain break-even, which matches the $3,000 target divided by the 60% margin ratio exactly.

Example 3 — a price that can never break even. Price $8, variable cost $9. Contribution margin is −$1: every unit sold loses a dollar before a cent of fixed cost is covered, so no sales volume, however large, reaches break-even. The calculator flags this immediately instead of returning a technically-correct but meaningless negative "units" figure.

Reading the price sensitivity table

Price is usually the input a business has the most control over, and it's also the most sensitive one — because it moves both the numerator and denominator of the "units needed" question at once. Raising price grows the contribution margin per unit, which shrinks the number of units needed to break even; cutting price does the reverse, often sharply, since a small price cut can shrink an already-thin margin by a large percentage. The built-in sensitivity table recalculates the break-even point at five price points from −10% to +10% around your entered price, holding variable cost and fixed costs constant, so you can see at a glance how much a discount or a price increase would move your required sales volume.

Contribution margin vs. gross margin vs. net margin

MetricWhat it subtracts from price/revenueWhat it's used for
Contribution marginVariable costs onlyBreak-even and target-profit volume
Gross marginCost of goods sold (variable + some fixed production cost)Production and pricing efficiency
Net marginAll costs, including overhead, interest, and taxOverall profitability of the business

Break-even analysis specifically needs the contribution margin, not gross or net margin, because it's built on separating costs into fixed (don't change with volume, like rent or salaries) and variable (change with each unit sold, like materials or a per-order shipping fee) rather than by accounting category.

Limitations worth knowing before you rely on the number

  • Costs are treated as perfectly linear. In reality, large volume changes can shift fixed costs in steps (a second shift, more warehouse space) or change variable cost per unit (bulk discounts, rush-shipping surcharges) — the calculator assumes today's cost structure holds across the range you're testing.
  • One product at a time. The math here is per product or per SKU; a business selling several products needs either a blended average contribution margin or a separate break-even run for each product line.
  • Demand isn't part of the model. Break-even tells you the volume required to stop losing money — it says nothing about whether the market will actually buy that many units at that price.
  • It's a planning tool, not accounting output. Treat the result as a planning estimate rather than a substitute for a full costing exercise done with an accountant.

Frequently asked questions

What is the break-even point, and how is it calculated?

The break-even point is the number of units you must sell for total revenue to exactly equal total costs — the point where profit is zero. The formula is Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price per unit − Variable cost per unit); the denominator is called the contribution margin. For example, with $5,000 in fixed costs, a $25 price, and a $10 variable cost, the margin is $15 per unit, so you need 5,000 ÷ 15 ≈ 333.33 units — rounded up to 334, since you can't sell a third of a unit. Break-even revenue is Fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio (60% here) ≈ $8,333.33.

What is contribution margin, and why does it matter?

Contribution margin is what's left from each unit's price after covering its variable cost — the amount that "contributes" toward paying off fixed costs and, once those are covered, toward profit. It's shown both per unit (price minus variable cost) and as a ratio (margin divided by price), which tells you what percentage of every dollar of revenue is available to cover overhead. A higher contribution margin means fewer units are needed to break even and each additional sale adds more to profit; a thin margin means volume has to be much higher before the business turns a profit.

What happens if my price is lower than my variable cost per unit?

If the price per unit is less than or equal to the variable cost per unit, the contribution margin is zero or negative — every unit you sell loses money before fixed costs are even considered, so there is no volume of sales that reaches break-even. This calculator detects that case and shows a clear warning instead of a misleading number. The fix isn't more volume; it's raising the price, cutting the variable cost per unit (materials, packaging, shipping, payment fees), or both, until price exceeds variable cost.

How do I calculate the units needed to hit a specific profit target, not just break even?

Open the profit target field and enter the profit you want for the period; the calculator adds it to your fixed costs before dividing by the contribution margin: Units needed = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution margin per unit. This is the same break-even formula with a profit cushion added to the costs side, which is why break-even is really just the special case of a target profit of zero. The tool also shows the revenue needed to reach that target so you can sanity-check it against realistic sales volume.

Is my cost and pricing data sent to a server?

No. Every number you enter — fixed costs, price, variable cost, and profit target — is calculated entirely in your browser and only saved to your own device's local storage so your last entry is there next time. Nothing is uploaded, logged, or shared, so you can model real pricing and cost figures without exposing them.
