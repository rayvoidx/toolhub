Adding VAT and removing VAT are different sums

Adding VAT is a multiplication: take the net price, multiply by the rate, and add the result to the net price. Removing VAT from a gross price is a division, not a subtraction, because the VAT was originally calculated on the smaller net figure, not on the larger total you are holding now. Subtracting the rate straight from the gross price is the single most common VAT error, and it always understates the net price.

The gap is small on a coffee and material on an invoice. At 20% VAT, subtracting the rate from a gross price of 10,000 gives a net figure of 8,000 when the correct answer is 8,333.33 — a 333.33 difference that will not reconcile with an accounting system expecting the exact division.

Worked example 1: adding VAT to a quote

A freelance invoice for a net fee of 850.00 at the standard German rate of 19% needs VAT of 850 × 0.19 = 161.50 added on top. The gross amount the client pays is 850.00 + 161.50 = 1,011.50. Selecting "Net price (before VAT)" and tapping the 19% chip reproduces this instantly.

Worked example 2: reclaiming the net amount from a receipt

A receipt shows a gross total of 240.00 at 20% VAT and you need the net figure for an expense report. Switch the mode to "Gross price (with VAT)": 240.00 ÷ 1.20 = 200.00 net, with VAT of 40.00. The division undoes the original multiplication exactly, which subtraction never does.

Worked example 3: comparing a price across two VAT rates

A supplier quotes 500.00 net for the same goods whether the buyer is in a 21% or a 5% VAT jurisdiction. At 21% the gross price is 605.00; at 5% it is only 525.00 — an 80.00 gap driven entirely by the tax rate, not the underlying price. Comparing gross totals without checking the VAT rate behind them can make an identical deal look more or less expensive than it is.

Common mistakes and edge cases

  • Subtracting instead of dividing: to go from gross to net, divide by (1 + rate), never subtract the rate as a percentage of the gross figure — the two only agree when the rate is exactly 0%.
  • Confusing the rate with the multiplier: a 20% rate corresponds to a ÷1.20 (or ×0.8333) reverse calculation, not ÷1.2 rounded to a different figure — small rounding shortcuts compound on large invoices.
  • Zero-rated versus exempt: zero-rated goods are taxed at 0% (net equals gross) while exempt goods fall outside VAT entirely — the distinction affects whether a VAT-registered business can reclaim input tax on related purchases.
  • Reduced rates on specific goods: many countries tax food, books, children's clothing, or medicine at a lower rate than the standard one, so the same country can have several correct answers depending on what is being priced.

Frequently asked questions

How do I add VAT to a net price?

Multiply the net price by the VAT rate to get the VAT amount, then add it to the net price for the gross price. At 20% VAT, a net price of 100.00 gives VAT of 100 × 0.20 = 20.00, so the gross price is 120.00. Pick "Net price (before VAT)", enter the amount and the rate — or tap one of the rate chips (5, 7, 10, 15, 19, 20, 21, 25) for common VAT/GST rates — and the net, VAT, and gross amounts update instantly.

How do I remove VAT from a gross price (reverse VAT)?

Divide by (1 + rate), don't subtract the rate as a percentage of the gross price — that is the classic reverse-VAT mistake. At 20% VAT, a gross price of 120.00 is 120 ÷ 1.20 = 100.00 net, with VAT of 20.00. Subtracting 20% straight from 120.00 would wrongly give 96.00 net, a 4.00 error. Switch this VAT calculator to "Gross price (with VAT)" and enter the total to see the correct pre-VAT figure.

Which VAT rate should I use?

VAT and GST rates vary widely by country and by product category, so check your local tax authority for the exact figure. As a rough guide, the rate chips cover common standard rates: 5% (Canada's GST), 7% (Thailand), 10% (Australia's GST, Japan's consumption tax), 15% (South Africa, New Zealand's GST), 19% (Germany), 20% (the UK, France), 21% (Netherlands, Belgium, Czechia), and 25% (Sweden, Denmark, Norway). Many countries also apply reduced rates to food, books, or medicine, so double-check before relying on the standard rate.

Does this VAT calculator work with any currency, and is my data sent anywhere?

Yes to both. Adding or removing VAT is pure percentage math with no currency conversion involved, so the result is correct whether you're entering dollars, euros, yen, or any other unit — just type the number without a currency symbol. Everything runs locally in your browser: the amount and rate you enter are never uploaded or stored on a server, and there's no sign-up or tracking.

What happens if I set the VAT rate to 0%?

At 0% VAT the net and gross prices are identical and the VAT amount is zero — useful for zero-rated goods and services, which are taxable at 0% rather than exempt (the distinction matters for VAT-registered businesses reclaiming input tax). Enter 0 in the rate field, or as a custom rate if it isn't one of the presets, to confirm a price shouldn't change after VAT.
